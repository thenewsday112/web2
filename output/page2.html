Aliens, Bigfoots, and Monsters… Remembering the Age of the Weirdumentary

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
“Scientists representing the world’s foremost research centers took part in the examination of the evidence. The facts that will be presented are true. This may be the most startling film you’ll ever see.” -Peter Graves, The Mysterious Monsters (1975)

During the turbulent era of Vietnam and Watergate, the happy veneer of America in the 1950s and ‘60s gave way to the conspiracy-minded ‘70s. Dark revelations about the government, justice system, military, and police made the public begin to rethink their perceptions about a great many things. This led to renewed interest in occult matters like ESP, UFOs, and cryptozoology. These subjects, along with other pseudoscientific phenomena like near-death experiences or the Bermuda Triangle, became the bread and butter of a Utah-based production house called Sunn Classic Pictures. The company made a fortune releasing partially-dramatized “documentaries” that tantalized viewers with possibilities of what could be out there beyond the veil.

“I think it was people just questioning what the norm was… what is real, what isn't real,” James L. Conway, who worked as a writer/producer/director at Sunn Classic, tells IGN. “There were very few outlets back then to experience these things, very few TV channels. Through a theatrical window, we were able to make movies about these subjects. They were incredibly popular because there was no other place for people to get knowledge about this… no YouTube, no Wikipedia, no social media. If you wanted to see something that was on the unusual side, it was a theatrical motion picture.”

Conway was a college senior at the University of Denver around 1971 when he was in the Mass Communications Department and the phone rang. It was Charles E. Sellier Jr., who ran an industrial film and commercial company in Denver.

Continues Conway, “Chuck was looking for cheap labor to edit his industrial films. I said, ‘I'm your guy.’ While I finished college I worked for Chuck, and after about three months I said to him, ‘You know, I can direct these shows better than the people you're hiring.’ Total chutzpah. He gave me a shot, I did really well, and that began our relationship.”

After producing a western comedy called The Brothers O'Toole, Sellier connected with Sunn Classic Pictures, which at that point was a distribution outfit in Utah and a newly-founded division of the Schick razor company. They had made several well-attended hunting pictures like The Life and Times of Grizzly Adams, and decided their next avenue would be a series of pseudoscience documentaries kicked off by a re-edited and English dubbed version of the 1970 German doc Chariots of the Gods. Based on Erich von Däniken’s popular claptrap supposing celestial beings who seeded early humanity, in 1973 it grossed over $25M and was nominated for an Oscar.

'From the late ‘60s we see a rise in interest in everything from occultism and psychic phenomena, new age philosophies, magic, astrology, paganism, life after death, anti-materialism, holistic medicine, reincarnation…'

Noted film scholar Dr. Gary D. Rhodes recently published a book titled Weirdumentary all about this era of strange docs throughout the ‘70s, of which he claims Chariots of the Gods is the urtext: “The fact that it was nominated for an Academy Award suggests how many more people were taking these ideas seriously. I hesitate to think a documentary on that subject now would get an Academy Award nomination. From the late ‘60s we see a rise in interest in everything from occultism and psychic phenomena, new age philosophies, magic, astrology, paganism, life after death, anti-materialism, holistic medicine, reincarnation… there's a desire to find alternate ways of examining our experiences that is unique to that era, including academia. You would not find many parapsychology departments in today's world.”

Once they found the winning formula, Sunn Classic released a wave of documentaries including 1975’s The Mysterious Monsters, where Mission: Impossible actor Peter Graves hosts a Whitman’s Sampler of cryptids.

“For me as a little kid, I certainly loved Mysterious Monsters the most of any of them because it was the era of fascination with Bigfoot, widespread belief that there might be such a creature,” says Rhodes. “There was equal interest in the Loch Ness monster and the Yeti. There were documentary films individually about all of those monsters, but here it was like the Universal monster rally films where you were getting all of them at once.”

The film was actually an amalgam of a few previously-released TV documentaries including CBS’ Monsters! Mystery or Myth, which helped popularize the supposed veracity of the Roger Patterson/Robert Gimlin footage of Bigfoot strolling through the woods. Narrates Graves, “If this film is legitimate, then there can no longer be any question about Bigfoot’s existence.”

Because Sunn Classic was a Mormon-run company, Rhodes says they “wanted to tap into films where everybody from a little kid to the grandparents could go and not feel out of place or offended.” Sellier himself called this demographic “the lost audience” who felt left behind by both the kiddie fare of Disney and the blood-and-nudity-soaked visions of New Hollywood auteurs (Taxi Driver, The Exorcist, etc.). Sunn’s product even included a few movies which attempted to present scientific evidence for aspects of the Christian faith. The most successful was In Search of Noah’s Ark, co-written and directed by Conway based on a book by Sellier and David W. Balsiger. It posits that (conveniently inaccessible) evidence exists that the remains of Noah's Ark lay on Turkey’s Mount Ararat. It includes several non-Russell Crowe re-enactments, as well as a supposed photograph that became the lynchpin for “proof.”

Intones narrator Brad Crandall, “The answer came in late September of 1974 when an expedition from the Holy Ground Mission in Frankston, Texas, scaled Mount Ararat, shooting across the Ahora Gorge with a 35MM Minolta camera. They took this picture that shows what looks like the fore part of a great vessel. The prow can be seen sticking out of the rocks 2000 feet above the location of the Eryl Cummings photo. When studied under photographic enlargement, the outline of the ship is not only clear but planking can actually be seen… the planking of Noah's Ark.”

“We didn't doctor anything… we had a photo that had been in other pieces of research,” Conway explained to us. “In those days, every expert you go to has their own data to support what they believe. Whether it's pseudoscience or not, all these people had books or research they could point to proving their point. A lot of it is things people wanted to believe, and so they'll accept the research more readily than a scientist might. I actually just saw there was a report on the Today Show last week about how they found this thing near Mount Ararat that they think is Noah's Ark. It just never goes away.”

Adds Rhodes of the questionable flood geology, “There's something atop Mount Ararat, and we think it's wood, and then this one explorer went up and claims to have seen it, and another explorer got close, but he couldn't quite get there. For some people, that's still tantalizing.”

Sunn Classics had several innovations to make and market their movies. They would often spend tens of thousands doing market research on whether a subject was viable for public interest. Once a film was complete, they would circumvent Hollywood distribution through a practice known as four-walling.

They’d say, 'We're going to pay you $25 cash for Friday night, $25 cash for Saturday night. We control the box office. Everything that comes in is ours.'

Conway claims that when Sunn approached local theaters, “They’d say, ‘We're going to pay you $25 cash for Friday night, $25 cash for Saturday night. We control the box office. Everything that comes in is ours.’ Because it was four-walled we did very heavy television commercials. We would only have, let's say, 300 prints of the movie. It would go into a city like Chicago, play two nights at this one theater, three nights at a different theater, four nights at another theater, and the commercials would list all the different movie theaters it was going to be on each night. They were enormously successful. On In Search of Noah's Ark I think our budget was $600,000 and it grossed $55 million. It was the 9th highest grossing movie of 1976.”

“Four-walling was a brilliant strategy,” opines Rhodes. “Sunn Classic were, in some respects, using old tricks of the trade not unlike the roadshow cinema of the early 20th century. In other respects, they were way ahead of their time. They were using surveys, polling at shopping malls: ‘What kind of film do you want to watch?’ They would look at advance weather reports in tandem with four-walling to try and avoid a snowstorm in the weeks ahead.”

“It may start in the Bible Belt, but it was huge everywhere you went,” continues Conway. “It was a huge hit, quite remarkable. That taught us that if you have a great commercial hook you can get a great box office return. That's where a lot of the supernatural elements come in, because there was an appetite for it. It's very easy to do a 15- or 20-second hook in a commercial to get people's interests up. It was so successful that theater runners made us go back to the standard splitting the box office, which was bad for us, good for them.”

Another “bad for us, good for them” was producer Alan Landsburg bringing the formula to television with an In Search of… docuseries hosted by Leonard Nimoy from 1976 to 1982. Landsburg helped popularize the style with the earlier docs In Search of Ancient Astronauts (1973), In Search of Ancient Mysteries (1974), and The Outer Space Connection (1975), the latter made and released by Sunn Classic. The show’s subjects (Extraterrestrials, Magic & Witchcraft, Missing Persons, Myths & Monsters, Lost Civilizations, Special Phenomena) were the same as Sunn’s, as was the format of a celebrity host.

“He came after us with the In Search of… television series, basically ripped off our In Search of Historic Jesus and In Search of Noah's Ark,” says Conway. “He made it a TV series that was very successful, but that came after we had done our stuff.”

Many others were tapping into the same material, including the Oscar-winning insectsploitation doc The Hellstrom Chronicle (1971) and the Rod Serling-hosted UFOs: Past, Present, and Future (1974), but Rhodes says that “Sunn Classic was at the core of the weirdumentary.” The one defining aspect of all these sensational films was selling the audience on realms of existence which, whether truthful or not, absolutely fall into the category of “fun to believe.”

“Belief was not essential,” Conway says of his Sunn projects, which included The Lincoln Conspiracy (1977) and Beyond and Back (1978). “Some I believed in more than others. An example is Beyond and Back, which we did about life after death and near-death experiences. As I was interviewing all these different people, I became more of a believer in it. Some of the other things we did, the more people I talked to the less I might have actually believed it, but you still present it.”

Although Sunn had researchers on staff, the scientific accuracy of these movies was debatable. It seems the real science lay in how the movies were made, or – as Conway explains – edited: “We were one of the first people to do a lot of audience testing. We would make a film, and then we would screen it and people would have dials. If they like something they would go right, didn't like it, go left. We would actually adjust the editing based on if something just completely sucked.”

Much of the filmmaking in the dramatizations of Sunn movies is very effective, with Rhodes stating that “Beyond and Back shows the ingenuity of Conway because at times they were actually able to muster really good special effects on low budgets.” Another example is their take on Charles Berlitz’s wildly popular supermarket paperback The Bermuda Triangle (1979), where the famous disappearance of Flight 19 in 1945 – linked to UFOs – is told with eerie lighting and sound effects that transcend the cheapness.

Still, critics were not typically kind, with Variety stating of The Bermuda Triangle, “As with the other Sunn films, recollections from people, both living and dead, who have experienced the phenomenon in question, are reenacted in a silly, unconvincing fashion. So-called experts with degrees and credentials are carted out in front of the camera to add weight to the arguments. Brad Crandall narrates, tying together the bits and pieces.” Yet as Rhodes writes in his Weirdumentary book, “To lesser men we leave these petty slights. As I see it, Sunn had perfected an art form, a very profitable art form, crystallizing a new genre for a new age.”

Eventually conspiracies and psychic phenomenon and weird creatures were no longer controversial, they were part of the norm… hence no longer a viable attraction for theaters. Big movies like Close Encounters of the Third Kind (which tied elements of Bermuda Triangle lore into UFO mythology), Carrie (telekinesis a-go-go), and The Amityville Horror (ghostly possession) made formerly exotic pseudoscience mainstream… and perhaps less exciting. Eventually Sunn and Conway moved on to fictional feature films like Hangar 18 or The Boogens before eventually being folded into the now-defunct Taft Entertainment, acquired by Aaron Spelling and finally Paramount.

'Once Hollywood realized how popular these subjects were, they were suddenly everywhere.'

“Once Hollywood realized how popular these subjects were, they were suddenly everywhere,” said Conway. “They were TV movies, they were episodes. The Landsburg television show was on every week exploring another mystery from this, that, or the other. Once it became more easily available, there wasn't as much hoopla. When they first happened, they fed on each other… it gave you ink in magazines and newspapers. They kept feeding it and finally ran out of things to exploit. You can only do so many ghost stories, so many UFO things. The first time you're exposed to it, it's all fresh and fascinating and leads you down a lot of rabbit holes. Rabbit holes are much easier now than they used to be to go down.”

Even though we live in an information age, audiences continue to buy into the myths perpetuated during the Weirdumentary era, as exemplified by the continued popularity of such pseudoscience programs on Discovery or The History Channel, covering everything from UAPs to skinwalkers. There’s also a legion of podcasts and blogs which dedicate whole episodes to, say, disappearances in national parks or the history of ghost sex. Ancient aliens is a mini-industry unto itself, despite von Däniken’s work having long ago been exposed as fraudulent. Although it was thought to be a vanished victim of the Bermuda triangle, the SS Cotopaxi was eventually identified in January 2020 off the coast of St. Augustine, Florida. Even the famous Patterson-Gimlin footage of Bigfoot seen in The Mysterious Monsters has now been debunked in this year’s documentary Capturing Bigfoot, where verified rehearsal footage of their Bigfoot costume is revealed. For some, even this has not been enough to dissuade people from believing in the footage.

“Certainly as kids these subjects resonated because you are more naïve,” says Rhodes. “There's always one more piece of possible evidence to be tantalizing. ‘Even if one thing was faked, that doesn't mean it's all fake. What about these others?’ There is a kind of whataboutism. If some UFO footage was fake or a photograph was fake, now we have Air Force footage that's been released that looks – perhaps to some – compelling. We have new Bigfoot recordings, so even if Patterson-Gimlin faked it, that's just their footage. Some of the shows I've been on – Coast to Coast or whatever – the people that speak to me are more interested in the topics than the actual films as films, right?

“From a historical film perspective, these movies are fun,” concludes Rhodes. “There's a nostalgia factor. James Conway, Charles Sellier, Alan Landsburg, Wheeler Dixon… they're heroes to me. They made incredibly fun movies, even if I disbelieve the subjects. They're wonderful.”

Avatar: Seven Havens Showrunners Address Your Biggest Finale Burning Questions

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Full spoilers follow for Avatar: Seven Havens Season 1!

The first Book of Avatar: Seven Havens is, er, in the books with this week’s finale, “The Root Chakra.” And while the final four episodes answered a number of big questions, they raised a ton more — not just about what to expect in Season 2 of the Paramount+ animated series, but Seasons 3, 4, and perhaps beyond. It’s a good thing, then, that we have show co-creator Michael Dante DiMartino and showrunners Sehaj Sethi and Ethan Spaulding on hand to answer all your burning questions.

…Or at least, answer what they can. While we got to see Pavi (Saheli Khan) step up as the Avatar in the finale, unlock her Root Chakra, and head off on a classic Avatar journey to the Floating Isles next season, Nisha (Aishu Devan) — surprisingly — was not part of that unlocking and is staying (for now) in the Haven of Ellora. Not only that, but we also saw Nisha was at least in part the cause of the Fear Anomaly that’s been attacking Ellora all season long, activated when her parents abandoned her; as a side effect, she accidentally killed her parents in a rock slide.

But there are still the questions of why and how Avatar Korra caused a Cataclysm 225 years earlier, how there are twin Avatars now, and many more mysteries and surprises to explore. So let’s get into it and see what DiMartino, Sethi, and Spaulding can answer about the future of the Avatar franchise.

More: Avatar: Seven Havens Season 1 Finale Review and Recap

Remembering Eric Bauza

Before we get to that, though, earlier this week the world of animation suffered a shocking loss with the passing of actor Eric Bauza at age 46. Bauza voiced multiple characters in this season of Seven Havens, and given the pace of animation, we’ll likely hear his voice for several seasons to come. But his passing does leave a huge gap in the world of Avatar.

“Eric meant a lot, obviously, to the show,” Spaulding said. “He's all over Seven Havens, and his contribution to animation in general is so prevalent. He's going to leave a big hole.” Sethi continued, “He worked on this show, but we all have history with him beyond this show. He's worked on so many things. I joked all the time that Bauza was twenty percent of the population of Seven Havens. We used him because he was so brilliant. He could act against himself. He could do young, old, any voice you could think of… Every single time he was in that booth, he was an absolute delight… He was just the kindest person… It's just the biggest loss.”

But Bauza’s contributions stretch beyond Seven Havens.

“He was actually in Avatar: The Last Airbender,” DiMartino recalled. “That was when he was just getting started in his voice-acting career. I think he's like a guard or two in the Boiling Rock. So the fact that he's been part of this universe for so long… I knew he was a big deal, [but] hearing all the stories from people over the past week, just learning more about him and the impact he's had on the community and everyone he's worked with is, it's just very sad. And [we] wish his family the best.”

Pavi And Nisha… Separated?

To get into the show itself, it’s a surprise that Pavi heads off on the classic Avatar journey at the end of the season alongside airbender Jae (Major Curda) while Nisha stays behind in Ellora. That, of course, was always part of the plan.

“From the very beginning, we all wanted this to be a really organic story about two sisters,” Sethi said. “Yes, there's a lot of world-building. There's so much that's going on. But at the end of the day, they are two young girls who fight and have differing opinions. Now that we've spent a whole Book, a whole season, seeing them grow together… What is it going to be like now when they're apart? How are they going to grow apart from one another into their respective roles? That was really interesting to me.”

Added Spaulding, “Also, getting the characters out into the world so the audience can see… other locations, it's a cool thing to have the characters split up at some point in the storyline for the world-building.”

Welcome to the Floating Isles

Part of that world-building is the next Haven Pavi is headed towards: The Floating Isles. We’re told she senses trouble there, and it seems likely that’s the location of the next Chakra she needs to unlock. So what can they tell us about the place?

"We try to make every Haven they're going to visit its own mini-culture [with] different customs and everything."

Not much. “That's Haven Number Two, and Pavi will be visiting there,” DiMartino confirmed. Calling it a “more chill place compared to Ellora,” which was greeted by knowing laughter from Spauling and Sethi, DiMartino continued, “It's definitely a different vibe culturally. We try to make every Haven they're going to visit its own mini-culture [with] different customs and everything. So it'll be fun to start seeing more of the world as we get out there.”

There Will Be More Than One Haven Per Season

Given Book 1 was entirely in Ellora, one might venture that Book 2 will be entirely in the Floating Isles. This would mean that either the show is going seven seasons or we’re going to go a little faster than we did in Season 1; turns out, it’s the latter.

“No, not exactly,” DiMartino said when asked about the ‘one Haven per season’ idea. “We had to split it up a little differently, but we'll get through all the Seven Havens by the end of this series for sure.”

Beyond that, they confirmed that the four seasons of the show is the entire series. “That’s the full story,” DiMartino said. Added Sethi, “When Mike, Bryan [Konietzko], and I started working on it, we always had an end goal in mind — a lighthouse for us to work the story towards — and that's always been the case throughout the series.”

Is Pavi the “Real” Avatar?

Apologies in advance, because the answer to this one won’t be satisfying, but we did ask about the revelation that Pavi’s chakras are messed up and she is able to unlock the Root Chakra alone but not with Nisha. So is repairing the Cataclysm solely up to Pavi? Is she the “real” Avatar, and is Nisha a side effect or something else?

“We’ll see,” Sethi said enigmatically. “You gotta keep watching, man. I promise all of those questions are answered.”

Hey, we tried!

Going Dark

One of the darkest moments in Avatar history is when it’s revealed that not only did Nisha’s parents abandon her, but in the process of summoning the Fear Anomaly, she accidentally killed them in a rock slide. While Sethi confirmed that there’s more to this story to come, it’s still a horrifying duo of revelations that skew way darker than fans were likely expecting.

“I don't want to speak for Mike or Ethan, but when we started the series, I always had a feeling this is going to be darker,” Sethi said. ”We were really tackling some difficult and complex situations. The amount of power these girls wield… I always was interested in the idea of putting very young characters in very mature positions. I thought there was something really interesting there, especially from a storytelling point of view. We're really forcing the girls to grow up in a lot of ways, and so for me that was just an interesting place to go as a storyteller. For the show and for where we are taking the story, it made sense to start dividing the girls on not just a philosophical level but an emotional level, and this is the biggest emotional divide you can give two characters. So to me, I really wanted to explore that in the upcoming season.”

Added DiMartino, “In the early development that Bryan and I did, we had [the] idea of this tragic backstory that happened with Nisha, the family, and Pavi. That's how she lost her leg. So the core of that ended up being what's in the show. We always knew that was going to be a big part of the mystery of Book One and a big reveal, and like you said, it's a dark moment. But it's totally necessary for their characters and the story, and so it all ties together. And yeah, there's more that we will dig into around that story in upcoming seasons.”

Korra Constellations

During a climactic moment in the finale when Pavi unlocks the Root Chakra, she also sees a vision of four past Avatars: Kyoshi, Aang, Roku, and finally, Korra. While Spaulding explained this is a nod to the constellation versions of the Avatars seen previously in both The Last Airbender and Legend of Korra, there seems to be a little more to it. And no, it’s not Pavi reconnecting with the Avatar Cycle after Korra broke the connection towards the end of Legend of Korra.

“It’s something else,” said DiMartino. “She has not reconnected. It can't happen that easily. So she does have a connection with Korra, but the other stuff's more of a vision.”

Added Sethi, “That's something we really had to work [on showing] visually that the connection is not complete. It was not just, oh, let's show Korra for a second or something. It was like, hey, she's sort of faded, right? She's not completely there. We wanted it to not just be so easy as, yup, now Korra can talk to you. That's something that still needs to progress in the series.”

Fans did notice in earlier episodes that both Korra and Asami are wearing betrothal necklaces in a “mover,” while in a vision of Korra where she’s tearing down Republic City, she is not. So how worried do we need to be about Asami?

“Asami is definitely dead by our show, which I think is a fair thing to say.” Sethi said. “The main thing to know from that is that there is most likely a time jump. But as to their relationship, you do learn more about that in Season 2.”

More LGBTQ+ Relationships in Seven Havens

One huge leap forward for the franchise is the amount of LGBTQ+ representation on screen. The final episode of Legend of Korra pretty famously had some issues in showing Korra and Asami getting together back in 2014. In 2026? Not only are the duo clearly together (and maybe betrothed), but we’ve also followed a canonically gay couple in Jae and Datu (Topher Ngu), and at least one other female-female pairing has shown up on screen.

“I definitely think that TV [and] even the kids’ animation landscape has definitely changed,” DiMartino reflected. “Whereas on Korra, we ran into some… pushback about what we could and couldn't show, this time around everyone was super supportive and it's more about the storytelling. What is the best way to show these relationships? It's nice to see, and we will see more of what Korra and Asami's relationship was like in the past.”

Return of the Afflicted

Despite Pavi unlocking the Root Chakra, things aren’t quite settled in Ellora, particularly since Chancellor Kayoko (Jennifer Sun Bell) is still clearly possessed by the Fear Anomaly as the season ends. So what’s going on here? Is the Root Chakra still blocked?

“The afflicted stay afflicted until a certain point,” Spaulding explained. “So there's still something that the Avatar has to do.”

Added Sethi, “Yeah, their journey is not finished with just releasing that energy. There's more that they'll need to do.”

We’re Not Getting a Huge Time Jump (Probably [Yet])

Fans at New York Comic Con noticed some designs of Pavi and Nisha that seemed to depict their characters aged up significantly, but as Sethi clarified, “that was concept art. So whether there's a time jump, who's to say, but that was not a time jump piece of art. That was just concept art.”

Other than that, though, time will be progressing forward as it tends to do. “Within the span of a series, there's time in between episodes,” Spaulding explained. “It's not very big, but you could say they're traveling for weeks or months. It’s similar to Last Airbender.”

“I give those amazing young ladies so much credit that they really learned how to grow their performance as the characters got more complex."

“Yeah, now that they're on the road, [you] gotta account for the travel time in certain ways,” DiMartino added. “Time passes, but it's not like we're jumping forward years or anything.”

Instead, they’re letting the growing maturity of the characters come from subtle animation changes, like a loosening of Pavi’s hair in the finale and the vocal performances of the actresses.

“I give so much credit to Saheli, who plays Pavi, and Aishu, who plays Nisha,” Sethi said. “I give those amazing young ladies so much credit that they really learned how to grow their performance as the characters got more complex. They did a great job, and shout out to our amazing voice director Kristi Reed, who helped them with that.”

Seven Havens: Fury Road

One of the fun parts of Seven Havens has been picking out post-apocalypse tropes hidden between the Avatar easter eggs. From a Mad Max-style introduction to two other Havens, to a zombie movie-esque Afflicted infestation, the series has embraced the setting in a big way. And while Sethi conceded they don’t have a list of tropes (or movies) they’re trying to hit, it is very much baked into the premise.

“The thing that I always found interesting when Mike and Bryan brought me this idea was that it's almost like post-post-apocalypse,” Sethi said. “They've created a new city, right? They've essentially already done the work of rebuilding, and now it's almost like, oh, is there another impending apocalypse coming? So that was the interesting thing of [doing] some post-apocalypse stuff, but it can't feel like Scavengers Reign because they've rebuilt. There are new places. We tried to weave it in where it really made logical sense with where the story was going and where the world was.”

"The thing that I always found interesting when Mike and Bryan brought me this idea was that it's almost like post-post-apocalypse.”

Added DiMartino, “When you get into Book Two, though, and now that they're traveling, you're going to see more of the Harshlands and stuff. So that gives you more of the post-apocalyptic vibe, and why people aren't living out in these areas.”

Seven Havens Gets Political (or Not)

Despite the multi-year schedule of creating animation, Seven Havens has felt laudably and eerily relevant to our world today. Turns of phrase in the finale like “We can’t just hide from the scary bubble” and “freedom of choice is all I’ve ever wanted” work on multiple levels. So how does a staff aiming for a hopefully timeless children’s animated series also reflect what we’re feeling right now in 2026?

“In a lot of ways, we were trying to really uphold an ethos that a lot of the Avatar world has always upheld,” Sethi explained. “I don't know if it's us getting political or everything that is relevant about humanity just being political at this point. To be truly honest, these were things that we always wanted to talk about, and we always felt they were timeless. We just happen to be in a time where they are incredibly relevant now.”

Added DiMartino, “All the Avatar stuff we've done has some political bent to it, but we're trying to address themes that are pervasive throughout history, not just talking about the current political climate. These are things that societies and people have struggled with throughout civilization’s history.”

“For me, especially, the idea of fear of the other, freedom of choice,” Sethi continued. “These are things that absolutely matter and will always matter. Like Mike was saying, the fact that they matter right now… Honestly, it's kind of cool that people can watch our show and feel a relevance now. But even 20 years from now, I think they'll still feel that relevance.”

The Hardest Part of Writing Seven Havens

Now that the secret of the show is out — that each of the Seven Havens does indeed align with one of the Seven Chakras — it gives the story a very clear, mission-based structure. But what it also does is somewhat dictate where Pavi and Nisha need to go emotionally over the course of the four seasons. So how do the writers balance honoring the emotional truth of those character’s journeys versus “well, we’ve gotta hit this chakra next.”

Arkade is a PC Peripheral That Transforms Every Modern Shooter Into a Light Gun Game

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you miss the days of Time Crisis, Virtua Cop, or even Duck Hunt, then Arkade Gaming Gun is a PC peripheral that should be firmly in your sights. At this week’s Gamescom Asia x Thailand Game Show 2026, I managed to get a short hands-on demonstration with this motion-controlled gun controller that’s due to release sometime in 2027 and came away with a strong first impression despite taking a few moments to wrap my head around its unique control setup.

What’s great about the Arkade Gaming Gun straight off the bat is that its proprietary technology doesn’t require a webcam or Wii-style sensor bar to make it work with your display. It’s completely plug and play, you connect its charging dock up to your PC via USB-C, and it doubles as a wireless receiver for the gun itself. A device manager allows you to customise its button layout and calibrate the tracking performance, and away you go.

You basically cradle the Arkade in a two-handed grip. The D-Pad or WASD-style movement of your character is handled by a thumbstick jutting out the side of its chunky barrel – which pulses with customisable LED graphics like a billboard in a Blade Runner skyline – with a pair of buttons on the other side used for other moves like jumping, and controls at the rear for weapon-switching.

Otherwise the actual aiming is handled by physically pointing the gun itself at your target, and that’s where things became initially disorientating during my brief hands-on. Dropped into a demo of Prodeus, a boomer shooter from developer Bounding Box Software, it took a moment for me to get comfortable with strafing and aiming at the same time. This was largely because to activate the aiming ability of Arkade you need to lightly grip the capacitive touch surface of its trigger. Rest your finger on the trigger and the onscreen crosshair will move to whatever target that you aim the gun at. Take your finger off the trigger, however, and you’re basically locked in the direction that you’re facing, as though you were playing with a mouse and keyboard and suddenly slipped your hand off the mouse.

Once I did get a feel for it, though, it largely became second nature. I even started instinctively re-centring myself on the fly with subtle movements of my trigger finger, not unlike when you’re aiming with a mouse and you’re pushing towards the edge of the mouse pad, and you pick it up and place it back in the middle without really thinking about it. It’s a fairly tricky thing to explain, but basically once it clicks it feels incredibly fun to peer down the Arkade’s sights and blaze away at a room of Doom-style demons with no noticeable compromises to the freedom of your movements. I especially like how its trigger features two stages of operation – a half-squeeze basically mimics a right mouse-button by zooming in slightly on your target, while a full squeeze fires the shot.

[I was shown] a video of someone using the Arkade Gaming Gun in tandem with a racing wheel to execute drive-by shootings in Grand Theft Auto 5.

According to its creator and CEO Joshua Fuchs, the Arkade Gaming Gun isn’t solely designed for first-person shooters. Apparently it also supports a number of third-person shooters as well, from Fortnite to the Resident Evil 3 remake. It can also be used in conjunction with other peripherals to further increase the immersion, with Fuchs showing me a video example of one of his team members using the Arkade Gaming Gun in tandem with a racing wheel to execute drive-by shootings in Grand Theft Auto 5; one hand on the wheel and the other pulling the trigger on the Arkade. I’m not sure how much peripheral-juggling and custom controller-mapping would be required to play the entire game that way, but it certainly suggested that players could have a lot of fun getting creative with this device.

For now the Arkade gaming gun only supports PC, Mac, and Steam OS, but Fuchs has hopes of bringing it to consoles at some point in the future. The gun itself feels really solidly constructed, but light enough that I don’t imagine it would become too tiring to wield it for extended gaming sessions.

One thing that does feel a little heavy though, is the price. Arkade Gaming Gun is launching at $399 USD, although there is currently the option of scoring one at a discounted rate if you preorder from Arkade’s website. That’s a fairly hefty price to pay to bring a feeling of old school nostalgia to new school shooting, but if you’ve got the budget for it then based on my brief blast with it this light gun feels like it could be worth a shot.

By Skipping Last-Gen Systems, Stranger Than Heaven is Allowing RGG Studio to Fully Leverage the Power of Current-Gen Consoles at Long Last

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Stranger Than Heaven will apparently be the most technically accomplished game that developer RGG Studio has ever produced, and that’s largely due to the fact that it's being created solely for current generation hardware. The game’s producer, Hiroyuki Sakamoto, confirmed as much to IGN during an interview conducted at Gamescom Asia x Thailand Game Show 2026 this week.

“We set out to make Stranger Than Heaven as a completely brand-new game, with the goal of creating something entirely fresh while putting forth the absolute best quality we can produce right now,” explained Sakamoto. “[So] we decided to drop previous-gen platforms like the PS4 and focus on creating an experience best enjoyed natively on current-gen consoles like the PS5.”

Since the launch of the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S systems back in 2020, all of RGG Studio’s games have still kept one foot stuck in the previous console generation. 2024’s Like a Dragon: Infinite Wealth was developed to run across two eras of PlayStation and Xbox, and while Xbox One was finally dropped for Yakuza Kiwami 3 & Dark Ties earlier this year, support for PlayStation 4 remained.

However, by developing Stranger Than Heaven purely for PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X|S, and PC, the team hasn’t had to make any hardware-based compromises to its vision for the new game’s multiple decade-spanning journey through early 20th century Japan.

“In the past, hardware constraints meant limiting the number of characters we could display on screen at once, among other things we had to give up on,” continued Sakamoto. “With Stranger Than Heaven, by fully leveraging the PS5's capabilities we were able to showcase more characters on screen simultaneously than ever before and raise character quality to the next level.”

Despite some lingering reservations regarding its digital resurrection of the late rapper Tupac Shakur, IGN's Michael Higham found a lot to like about Stranger Than Heaven in his recent hands-on preview. With just over two months to go before its scheduled release in January, Sakamato seems supremely confident in where the game is at, going on to state, “I think we succeeded in achieving our goal of creating a richer visual experience with a stronger artistic identity than anything we've done prior.”

During the same chat, Sakamoto also confirmed the inclusion of in-game “cheats” in Stranger Than Heaven that you can read about here.
