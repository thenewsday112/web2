How To Unlock The Secret Ending In Phantom Blade Zero – True Ending Guide

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Are you ready to unlock the secret ending in Phantom Blade Zero? Every story has its conclusion, including your journey across the Wulin as Soul. That said, there are several requirements that you need to be mindful of, including important quests and points of no return. Our guide discusses every single detail but, because of the topic at hand, you can bet that there will be major spoilers below.

Phantom Blade Zero secret ending - The point of no return and important quests

The Phantom Blade Zero secret ending, also known as the true ending, requires you to complete optional story quests throughout the campaign. It’s pretty much an overarching narrative where you discover the Secrets at the Heart of the Lake, the Herald’s Illusion, and Inanis’s role.

These tasks must be completed before you reach the game’s point of no return: Mirage Estate. Here’s a quick summary:

  • After completing the main mission in Pangzhen, you learn that you have to go to the Mirage Estate. However, there’s no way to enter via the steps/thoroughfare.
  • The quest then changes to The Four Serpent Houses. This is when you discern that there’s a hidden passage behind the snake door in the Green Serpent House.
  • After collecting the items needed to open the doorway, you can continue onward to the Mirage Estate. However, if there are still important quests that you need to tackle, you’ll be warned that you have “unfinished business.”

You can still fast travel to other regions even if you prematurely enter the Mirage Estate. It’s just that the requirements for the true ending might fail, and you don’t want that to happen.

You don't want to enter the Mirage Estate if you still have unfinished story quests in your journal.

Early-game quests and overarching plots

There are early-game quests that you should tackle to unlock the true ending. These include Deep in the Valley, Crimson Manor, Bloodline Awakening/Long Pillars, and Quick Hand Zhang Treasures. (You can check out these guides we’ve linked for more detailed tips.)

Deep in the Valley grants you entry to the lower section of the first region, where you’ll find the Crimson Manor and Long Pillar. Quick Hand Zhang, meanwhile, has you collecting relics in various zones where he has a hideout.

From left to right: Deep in the Valley, Bloodline Awakening, and the Crimson Manor.

Secrets at the Heart of the Lake: The Village of Lu

The Phantom Blade Zero Secrets at the Heart of the Lake is an overarching quest that’s initially started in the Village of Lu.

  • First, you’ll want to collect the six Firefly’s Memory Flowers scattered all over the village. These allow you to enter an optional area near the Temple of the Blue Fireflies, the settlement with a bunch of friendly puppets.
  • Jump down the pit and defeat a boss named Dusk, the Puppeteer to receive rewards and trigger a cutscene.
  • If you defeat an acolyte/scarecrow in the farm fields to the north, you’ll also learn that the objective is part of the quest.
These Memory Flowers give you hints regarding a major quest... that's totally optional.

Exploration and collectibles: The Illusory Pipa, the Acolyte Masks, and the Herald’s Illusion

Certain quests, such as Illusion of Inanis and Sha-Chi-Powered Boat are completed as you continue to explore other regions. For instance, Sha-Chi-Powered Boat leads you to Meigu Lake. As for Whence the Weeping and Pillars of Kunlun, you receive these quests a few hours into the campaign, and you complete them upon clearing the Mountain of Imprisonment.

Other quests also play a major factor:

  • A good example is the Illusory Pipa, which lets you dispel impenetrable barriers that open up new areas for exploration.
  • There’s also The Herald’s Illusion, where you seek out the Three-Eyed Puppet’s domains.
  • Lastly, you should acquire all Acolyte Masks, as these items grant entry to a secret area.

Note: Completing these tasks nets you two Crystal Puppet Eye items (Yin and Yang).

From left to right: The Illusory Pipa, Herald's Illusion, and Acolyte Masks.

Secrets at the Heart of the Lake: The Meigu Lake Discordance and Inanis' Realm

The aforementioned Acolyte Masks are used to shatter the Four Guardians’ Mirror, a mystical location in the frozen lands in northwestern Meigu Lake. Explore the Discordance realm at your leisure and collect more loot. At the very end, you have to defeat the Blizzard Blademaster.

After the Blademaster’s fall, an ominous-looking statue will appear right in front of you. Interact with it and place the two Crystal Puppet Eyes to get transported to the Heart of the Lake. Kill all the cultists to stop their ritual, which then leads you to another encounter with Inanis. This completes the Secrets of the Heart of the Lake.

The two Crystal Puppet Eyes teleport you to the ritual site in the Heart of the Lake.

What happens when you enter the Mirage Estate and Soulrend Cliff

To summarize: If you want to get the secret ending in Phantom Blade Zero, you have to complete all story quests that aren’t tied to the main mission--i.e. those with a purple icon. Once you’ve done all of those, it’s time to enter the Mirage Estate. Doing so causes changes to certain locations, such as the Crimson Forest’s eastern part getting blanketed in snow.

Proceed deeper into the Mirage Estate, fighting enemies, taking out bosses, and picking up all sorts of loot. Along the way, don’t forget to complete the following:

  • Bloodline Awakening - The fifth and final Long Pillar is in the estate grounds.
  • Quick Hand’s Request - Quick Hand Zhang has another hideout in the sprawling complex.

Eventually, you’ll acquire the Tiger Cannon, a Phantom Edge that can blow up destructible walls. Use it to reach Soulrend Cliff, the last region in the game. There are still lots of enemies, bosses, and rewards in this area, so just keep forging ahead.

After defeating what’s supposed to be the final boss, it will be followed by yet another encounter. We don’t want to spoil what happens next, but it should be pretty clear that this is your last foe. Once you’ve vanquished your opponent, sit back, relax, and watch the cutscenes.

Discern Inanis' true intentions and proceed further in to the Mirage Estate and Soulrend Cliff.

That does it for our guide on how to get the true ending in Phantom Blade Zero. Keep in mind that you can still fast travel to other locations while you’re in the Mirage Estate and also while in Soulrend Cliff (just prior to the last bell).

You should be able to find other goodies in the game world, such as the Soul Temper materials for your weapons. Also, don’t forget to battle the Three-Faced Puppet as part of the Triple Threat quest. And, if you’re up for even more challenges, you can try 66 Days Mode after beating the game.

Join Soul as he faces numerous threats in the Wulin and be sure to check out more tips in our Phantom Blade Zero guides hub.

Stranger Than Heaven Features Optional “Cheats” to Make its Combat Easier

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Stranger Than Heaven, the upcoming street-brawler from the makers of Yakuza and Like a Dragon, will feature optional in-game assists to make its challenging new combat system more approachable, developer RGG Studio has confirmed to IGN in an interview conducted at Gamescom Asia x Thailand Game Show 2026 this week.

In IGN’s most recent hands-on with the game, my colleague Michael Higham described Stranger Than Heaven’s parry-based combat system as “demanding”, adding that “you really have to pay attention to attack animations not just for timing, but to make the correct call on the direction it's coming from.” This initially seemed like it could prove to be divisive among fans of the comparatively carefree button-mashing combo systems found in RRG Studios’ previous adventures like Like a Dragon: Pirate Yakuza in Hawaii and Lost Judgment, but it turns out that Stranger Than Heaven’s more complex combat system will only be as difficult as players want it to be.

“We knew mechanics like parrying would be challenging, but based on feedback from players who tried the hands-on demos at various game shows, like [Gamescom Asia], we’ve fine-tuned details like difficulty balance and control responsiveness,” said Hiroyuki Sakamoto, producer of Stranger Than Heaven. "We've also included difficulty settings and provided plenty of support features, such as cheat items that increase health regeneration. We believe that if we offer enough support for action-game beginners, it will give them the chance to play through to the very end.”

Sakamoto insisted that with some perseverance from players and the inclusion of a lengthy tutorial phase in the early stages of the game to help them, he hoped that most players will eventually start to enjoy the new mechanics. He also stated that the highest difficulty setting is "extremely challenging," so the more battle-hardened gamers will still be able to face a more unforgiving combat experience should they so choose. However, for players who perhaps want to purely focus on enjoying the multiple decade-spanning story and the showbiz-based side activities without mastering the rhythm of the combat, the addition of the cheat items should mean that all fans of RGG Studio’s games will be able to enjoy Stranger Than Heaven.

“Equipping those cheat items makes you much harder to defeat by automatically restoring health and providing other perks,” explained Sakamoto. “For beginners who still struggle with blocking, we’re prepared assistance items that let you clear encounters using basic left and right punches alone.”

There is one small catch to using these assist items, however, but it’s a fairly humorous one in typically cheeky RGG Studio style.

“You can obtain [the cheat items] right from the start of the game,” said Sakamoto. “But because they function as ‘handicap’ items and you wear it as a cloth/garment, anyone watching your gameplay screen will immediately notice you’re wearing them.”

So no activating cheats and pretending to be an absolute parry-beast on a livestream, then.

Stranger Than Heaven is set for release on PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X|S and PC on January 15, 2027.

Monster Hunter Outlanders Review So Far

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Few games have changed the trajectory of their respective series quite like Monster Hunter World, one of the biggest smash hits Capcom has ever created. World was a vibrant, expansive, and ambitious leap that paid off in spades. It makes sense, then, that eight years later the series would still be chasing that high. Even with the time that's passed, I find myself amazed that Monster Hunter Outlanders, essentially, manages to cram the experience of World into a mobile game. This mobile spinoff certainly isn't attempting to reinvent the wheel, but after six hours with the game, it's proving to be a surprisingly competent package, although I have some bubbling hesitations about the grind and encroaching gacha systems.

Anytime there's a mobile spinoff of a major series, the first question that comes to mind is how that game will be compromised, or truncated, from that full console or PC experience. And so often, those mobile games are simplifications of a larger idea, like Fire Emblem Heroes or Diablo Immortal. What I've found fascinating about Monster Hunter Outlanders so far is that it doesn't really simplify the overall gameplay loop and structure. Rather, it's sort of a reconfigured Monster Hunter that strings out progression to support the typical framework of a mobile game, one filled with nearly a half-dozen different currencies to keep track of at any given time. But it also very much feels like Outlanders wants players to engage with it like a full-fledged Monster Hunter, and even includes all the story exposition you'd expect to go along with it.

Like most Monster Hunter games, Outlanders begins with a big cinematic opening where a group of adventurers from the Adventurer's Guild are striking out to an uncharted part of the world – in this case, an island called Aesoland. This island is filled with Radiant species of monsters, beasts whose abilities have been enhanced by a mineral called Radiantite. This is, ostensibly, also the game's reasoning for creating new designs of classic series monsters and tweaking their abilities to match this game specifically, like the Pukei-Pukei focusing more on head or tail attacks, which are easier to target with Outlander's more streamlined lock-on system.

But I cannot stress enough how much Outlanders banks on the same formula as World; it's almost as if the same game was transposed onto different locations and story elements. Even the first area of the game, like World, is a heavily forested area with a lot of verticality for climbing or setting ambushes; intentionally designed to show you the ropes. If you've played any Monster Hunter game, you know that core gameplay loop of what to expect – you take missions or hunts, track down monsters in an instanced map, beat them, harvest materials, and then use said materials to craft more powerful equipment.

Complementing this, you have a moderately-sized open world that you can roam around and explore to take down smaller monsters, find crafting materials, and more. Outlanders also brings back many of the elements that have become mainstays since World: scoutfly trails to guide you to objectives, buildable objects on the map like towers and ziplines, and monsters that interact and fight each other like natural predators. All these elements click together just as well as they did in World, creating a compelling loop that feels like you're constantly doing something meaningful to that overall progress. Outlanders is also impressive in terms of presentation with environments that genuinely look stunning; playing on an iPhone 14 Plus, I had a consistent framerate with very few dips.

However, the differences start to surface as you dig deeper into the minutiae of how the game actually plays and what options are available in combat scenarios. Certain aspects of the combat have been streamlined to fit the mobile experience and accommodate a touchscreen control scheme instead of a controller (although there is controller support if you want it). Movement and actions aren't as intricate, although the game sports two different control methods: Adventure Mode and Classic Mode. The former is intended for new players and streamlines things down to three buttons, Attack, React, and Burst. From there, the game contextually handles a lot of what you're doing, like the React button allowing you to dodge when a monster attacks, or block if the time is right. Classic Mode, on the other hand, gives you full manual control, including traditional button-stringed combos.

It's proving to be a surprisingly competent package, although I have bubbling hesitations about the gacha mechanics.

What I've found so far, though, is that Classic Mode proves to be unfit for just touch controls, as it's too cumbersome to use all of these different options without a controller. Adventure Mode does, generally, feel good to use, but takes some of the vibrant friction out of the experience by streamlining so much. That means the ideal way to play this, if you can, is on Classic Mode with a controller – which then ultimately raises the question of why you wouldn't just play a console Monster Hunter. There are answers to that question, of course, like if you don't own anything else to play a Monster Hunter game on.

The other two major elements where Outlanders shifts that classic experience is with your ability to build a party of adventurers and how progression works. To try and fit the typical idea of a gacha game, Outlanders creates a group of distinct characters that take part in the story who you can then recruit and use as NPC companions. Recruiting means partaking in those traditional gacha pulls using currency called Rutacoins.

Each Adventurer has their own distinct moveset that builds on one specific weapon type, meaning playing as that Adventurer is different from just slapping any other weapon on your player-created character. This does create some incentive to try and get these various characters. Of course, the real key to this system is how easy or difficult it might be to pull Adventurers and upgrade them to be useful in combat. I was able to build a full four-person party fairly easily with the currency I earned from starting the game. Overall, I like the idea of being able to build a party with these distinct characters and play solo. The option is still there to group up with other players and friends, but the Adventurers make it easy to tackle quests and Contracts on your own. But I can already see hints of it being frustrating to pull repeat characters, let alone anyone new you might want down the line.

This leads to the area I'm not as hot on: the progression system. While almost all of the currency in the game is earnable from playing, as well as spending real money, I'm finding that the classic Monster Hunter progression loop feels a bit slower here. You need Zenny to pay for items, upgrades, and services. Rutacoins are for pulling characters and sidekicks (Palicos and the like). Research Points needed for the upgradable skill tree to unlock more buildable structures, for example. And as you might expect, there are additional materials necessary for upgrading a character or sidekick's level, unlocking their level cap, upgrading weapons, crafting items, and more.

Monster Hunter has always had a lot of elements to juggle in terms of progression, which is arguably the biggest barrier to entry for some people. In many ways, the series has been about busy work, sifting through menus, and meticulously crafting sets of armor and weapons. Those are elements of Monster Hunter I've always enjoyed, but even after a half-dozen hours, I'm feeling quite fatigued by the sheer amount of stuff in Outlanders. There's a lot of currency and whatnot to earn, and equally as much to figure out in terms of how to get things done, as well as hours of tutorials.

This has, ultimately, made the opening hours a bit more of a slog than something like Monster Hunter World or Rise. And even though I'm genuinely having a good time so far, my primary concern at the moment is how all that might affect the experience as a whole, especially as you get to the more difficult and complex hunts. I've played enough gacha games in my life to know that the fun can be brought to a grinding halt pretty quickly when resources become a wall you have to overcome.

I'm feeling quite fatigued by the sheer amount of 'stuff' in Outlanders.

Of course, it'll take time to make a final judgement, but my overarching sentiment on Outlanders right now is surprise: for how it still leans into that essential component of timing in combat, and for how uncompromised the visuals and world design are. Those insidious mobile elements that too many games fall into are still nipping at the edges, but I'm hoping the core experience is able to stave it off in the long-run. I'll be pouring more time into Monster Hunter Outlanders in the coming days for the full review coming soon.

Magic: The Gathering Designers Detail Reality Fracture’s Jace Arc, Echoed Pairs, and Secret Lair Bundles

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Magic: The Gathering’s Reality Fracture set is a Universes Within set after two Universes Beyond sets in succession, but it’s also a little different in that it showcases iconic Magic characters, spells, and more that have been transformed by Jace Beleren.

The Planeswalker has transformed the universe into how he feels it should be, and that means new versions of well-known cards in a sort of “What If?” scenario.

IGN sat down with Yoni Skolnik, former Magic pro and Principal Game Designer, and Meris Mullaley, Worldbuilding Design Manager, to talk about the set’s influences, what comes in a pack, and the Secret Lair bundle.

Magic’s Designers Talk Reality Fracture Influences

Alternate universes are nothing new in pop culture, but I was curious how much last year’s Marvel’s Spider-Man set influenced Reality Fracture. As you can imagine, the genesis of Reality Fracture comes from a much earlier set.

“Some of the ideas go back to Planar Chaos, a set from 2007 that had a lot of What If elements,” Skolnik revealed.

“That set focused on messing with Magic's color pie, putting effects in colors where you wouldn't normally expect them.”

“While we didn't want to return to that exact formula for a What If set, the concept of What If is very compelling as it lets you pay tribute to Magic's devoted fan base.”

“Mark Rosewater [Head Designer] was keen to try the concept again, and it ended up fitting perfectly with the conclusion to Jace's storyline.”

“When developing the Echoed Pairs, most of our learnings came from other Magic IP sets that featured multiple legends,” Mullaley added.

“We chose characters who were (or could plausibly be) active in this current moment of Magic’s timeline. Creatively, we also kept most characters on their home planes rather than showing a multiversal cast of characters arriving on Hexhaven to stop Jace.”

“This helped the story of Reality Fracture keep a narrower focus on the key characters and reduced the amount of “why is this person here” questions.”

“We also see three separate Planeswalker cards for Jace, and we took a similar approach as we did with legends from Edge of Eternities or The Lord of the Rings: Tales from Middle-earth, where we see a character at multiple points along their narrative. Each Jace represents a different point in his journey, reflecting his accumulation of power and readiness to enact his final plan.”

The Jace of the Set

So what about Jace? The blue-hued Planeswalker has been around since Lorwyn in 2007, but has been in the background of events in recent sets.

“In terms of game mechanics, Empower Jace was perfect for making our main character very present even if you only lightly interact with the set,” Skolnik explained.

“For new Magic players who want to go down deeper into the history of Jace, there are a few cards that are specifically stronger with Jace Planeswalker cards, to make the process of going to look back at old cards feel rewarding.”

“Flavorfully, I was very impressed with how the worldbuilding team navigated the character. For new players, we're drawing on timeless fantasy tropes like ‘Dark Wizard's Tower’ and ‘Former Ally Turned Foe’ to make the story approachable, while still having plenty of deeper lore references in the art, and a gripping conclusion to Jace's storyline in the web fiction.”

“It also helps that Jace is such a deeply blue character, which makes understanding his role go hand in hand with understanding blue's place in the color pie. One of the first things many people learn about Magic is that blue players are evil, so having an archetypally blue villain was a natural fit!”

You hear that, blue players? You’re the bad guys here. Still, sticking with multiple versions of cards, I asked about how the Echoed Pairs have been received. For newcomers, Echoed Pairs include normal and Echoverse versions of cards, for example Karn, Argent Defender, and Karn, Gilded Guardian.

“The technology of guaranteeing 1 card from an exciting subset per pack has been very well received since debuting in Dominaria with 1 legend per pack,” Skolnik reveals.

“Going up to 3 legends per pack in Reality Fracture was necessary to guarantee echoed pairs given our printers' constraints, and it made for very exciting packs that fans are loving.”

“I trust we'll one day find another place to deploy this technology of 3 cards in a pack that is linked somehow. This experimentation was not directly related to the 2027 pack changes, but together they show the breadth of problems our production teams face while balancing Magic design's need for constant novelty and reinvention against the reality of what it takes to print all the Magic product for our growing audience!”

Secret Lair Bundle Is Here To Stay

In the time since we spoke with the Wizards of the Coast team, the Reality Fracture Secret Lair Bundle has been delayed. We asked about whether it’ll be sticking around.

“Yes! Secret Lair Bundle will become a staple of Magic Multiverse sets and is expected to appear in those sets going forward.”

“Part of the impetus for launching these bundles was to consolidate Special Bundles into one incredible package that we could repeat set over set. The theming of Reality Fracture and how we explore Jace’s inner sanctum made a lot of thematic sense to use as the jumping-off point for this new product line.”

So, onto the big question then: as someone who can’t believe their luck by packing Craterclaw Colossus and Hexhaven Invigorator, I was curious what the team’s favorite cards from Reality Fracture were.

“I really love both Ajani Planeswalker cards,” Skolnik explained.

“Seeing Ajani Resolute in standard Life Gain decks has been great. A 2-mana Planeswalker is very tricky to balance, but with Planeswalkers such a big part of the set, I really wanted to try and print one here.”

“Ajani Unrelenting has been demolishing in draft, but I’m also excited to see it doing work in Commander decks built around Planeswalkers.”

Mullaley’s pick was spoiler-filled, so look away, lore fans.

“Vindictive Triumph depicts one of my favorite moments in the fiction, where [spoilers] Liliana resurrects her Echoverse doppelgänger as a lich and takes the fight to Jace. I read that moment in an early draft of the story and knew immediately it needed to be a card.”

The Mongoose Review

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Mongoose is in theaters now.

There’s a scene in the 1971 sci-fi cult classic The Omega Man where Charlton Heston’s helicopter is crash-landing, and just before the moment of impact it flies into an embankment just below the camera’s POV… where the craft’s explosion happens safely off-screen. It was a cheapo move in 1971 – they clearly didn’t really blow up the helicopter – and it’s a cheapo move in 2026, too, when Liam Neeson’s The Mongoose – now, somehow, playing at a multiplex near you! – pulls a similar stunt.

Sure, in The Mongoose the helicopter doesn’t even blow up off-camera, but that’s not the point (the crash-landing winds up being played for a laugh instead). What I’m talking about here is the threadbare sense that such a moment evokes for the viewer, the “a third of the budget of this movie must’ve gone to Liam Neeson” feeling that permeates the not-quite high-octane, not-quite thrilling picture.

And yet, there’s a likability factor wafting around this story, thanks largely to Neeson’s disarming “yeah, I am taking a third of this film’s budget, and I’m gonna have fun doing it” affectation as he drives a stolen hotrod – do we still call them hotrods? – across various states while on the run from the police due to a crime he did not commit. Along for the ride, so to speak, are Ving Rhames, Marisa Tomei, and Michael Chiklis, all cashing in less than a third of the budget, presumably, but hitting their marks efficiently enough to get this thing over the finish line in a “Maybe just catch it on Prime Video eventually” kind of way.

The Mongoose tells its story through a mix of the chase Neeson’s Ryan “Fang” Flanagan is involved in and flashbacks to the former Special Forces soldier’s time in Afghanistan, specifically when he and his comrades (Rhames’ Tanker, Marcus Thomas’ Munch, and Ryan Morgan's Wiz) were captured by the enemy. Further flashbacks eventually fill in why Fang is on the run; specifically, his ex-wife Tara’s (Marisa Tomei) fiancé – “Todd something or other,” Fang growls – turns out to be not a nice person, and when Fang attempts to help Tara move out of the guy’s place, violence erupts. Next thing he knows, someone’s dead, and he’s stolen Todd something or other’s fancy, and quite fast, Ford Mustang. Other than that, all he’s got is a bullet in his leg, a vow to never be imprisoned again after his experience in the war, and the Mustang's built-in AI assistant, named Helene. “Is this, like, Siri?” Fang – and maybe Neeson too when he first read the script – asks.

Neeson's Fang is surprisingly chill about this situation, as he clearly has a death wish, having decided that he is not going to allow himself to be taken alive.

Helene, like many of the other characters (“characters”) in the episodic The Mongoose, serves as a foil for the surprisingly at-ease Fang to toss one-liners at while out-driving the various police who are pursuing him by land and air. Fang is surprisingly chill about this situation, as he clearly has a death wish, having decided that he is not going to allow himself to be taken alive. As the chase drags on, he eventually finds temporary refuge through the help of some other vets, and soon Fang begins to take on a legendary man-against-the-system status, thanks no doubt to his war hero cred, as well as the “live” CNN coverage of the high-speed pursuit, which IRL TV personality Laura Coates awkwardly hosts.

On the side of the law, there’s Maurice Marvel Meredith as Major James Tilton and Chiklis as Major Walter Pope. Suffice to say, one’s the good cop and one’s the bad copy, and never the twain shall meet, but also, never the twain shall have anything interesting or realistic to do or say. Rhames, unfortunately, seems to be struggling to deliver his lines at times, even while his very presence brings a sense of good will to the film. Tomei, meanwhile, is always likable, but most of her scenes are essentially just her on the phone with Neeson’s character, even as she miraculously overcomes some seriously life-changing events over the course of 24 hours or so.

As for the Mongoose of the title, it’s the beach in Mexico where Fang and Tara had their best days together, and as such it’s the ideal that Fang has fixated on – his “retirement” – if he somehow can escape his current, dire situation. But what he also must escape is the guilt of the men he left behind in Afghanistan, and it is this thread that director Mark Vanselow somehow scores with by the time the credits roll, aided no doubt by some keenly picked, and timed, needledrops, and a couple of very cleverly edited shots in the final moments of the film. The Mongoose may be working just one level above a direct-to-video cheapie for a lot of its running time, but it’s still got some heart, too.
