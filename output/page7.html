Terrible Lizards, available now on XBOX Series X|S and Game Pass, is a first-person platforming game about escaping runaway dinosaur animatronics in an abandoned roadside attraction. That perspective was something we landed on pretty deliberately, through a process of both considering this particular game’s needs, as well as reviewing what we’ve loved about movement in other games.

When I think about the platforming games I’ve always enjoyed most, a lot of it comes down to rhythm. I like being able to move quickly, react to what’s coming, and string things together without constantly stopping to line everything up.

Jumping off the Shoulders of Giants

You can see that reactive flow really clearly in older 2D platformers like Donkey Kong Country or the early Mario games. With a limited screen in front of you, you don’t always have much time to plan. You’re reacting, trusting the mechanics, and finding a rhythm as you move through the level. That’s what I wanted to bring into Terrible Lizards, and the first-person perspective is a big part of bringing that satisfying, rhythmic, reactive gameplay to a 3D platformer.

Games like Mirror’s Edge and Dying Light are the obvious first-person platforming reference points, but we actually wanted Terrible Lizards to feel a little different moment to moment. We were chasing the tighter, more immediate game feel you get from games like Titanfall, Call of Duty, and Overwatch, where simply moving, jumping, and changing direction feels responsive and satisfying. You do see that kind of feel in first-person platforming games like Neon White, and that was closer to the balance we wanted: strong platforming mechanics built on top of controls that already feel good before you even start chaining moves together.

Floaty Empowerment

The controls needed to feel tight and immediate, but we also ended up giving the player a little bit of floatiness in the air. That mostly came from our aerial control system. Once you jump, you have the ability to adjust your trajectory before you land, and that became one of the most important pieces of the movement system.

For someone who isn’t especially confident with first-person platforming, it gives you room to correct yourself. If you jump too far, you can pull back. If you drift left or right, you can make a small adjustment. You have a little extra time to understand where you’re going and make the landing. At the same time, someone who gets really comfortable with the system can exploit that same freedom. They can make harder jumps, move through sections more efficiently, find faster routes, and occasionally bypass parts of a platforming sequence entirely.

That gave us something we were really happy with: a low skill floor, but a high skill ceiling. You don’t have to be amazing at platformers to play the game, but there’s a noticeably different way of moving through it once everything starts clicking. You can absolutely slow down, explore, look for collectibles, and work your way through the environments methodically. But once you understand the movement and start chaining together jumps, slides, wall runs, clambers, ledges, and swings without really thinking about each individual input, there’s a flow state to it that I find really satisfying.

No Fight, All Flight

We also made a very deliberate decision not to give the player weapons or other means to fight back. Your movement is really your main way of dealing with danger. When one of the creatures comes after you, the answer isn’t to turn around and shoot it. You have to move, and that made the monsters a natural way to test everything the player has been learning.

Sometimes that’s a direct chase. Sometimes a creature causes the environment around you to start collapsing and you have to survive. Other times you’re dropped into a more open arena where the monster is loose in the space with you and you have to complete objectives or figure out how to escape. Those moments let us use the same core movement mechanics in different ways without turning every encounter into the same kind of chase.

Between those moments, the game has more traditional platforming, exploration, environmental puzzles, and larger caverns where figuring out how to move through the space is part of the challenge. Then we can tighten things back down and put the player under pressure again. That variety was important to us. We didn’t want the whole game to be six hours of constant sprinting, and we also didn’t want movement to feel like something that only mattered in designated platforming sections.

It’s really the foundation of how you interact with the game. The story, collectibles, exploration, hunt arenas, and New Game Plus content all sit around that, but the movement is what everything keeps coming back to. Once players get comfortable with it, the hope is that they stop thinking about every individual mechanic and just start moving through the world naturally. That’s when the game shines, and obviously when something is chasing you, that familiarity with the movement starts to matter a lot more.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 to Remove Seoul Crush Footage in November 12th Update

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Just a week after the release of Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, the shooter has found itself in the midst of a controversy regarding its depiction of the Seoul crush. Activision and developer Infinity Ward have caught criticism regarding the inclusion of footage taken from the news coverage of the 2022 real-life disaster that resulted in almost 160 deaths.

According to the BBC, bereaved families have said that they were “appalled” to see the footage, which shows up in the opening moments of Modern Warfare 4’s single-player campaign. The scene itself is meant to kick off the in-universe invasion by North Korea. Activision has apologized and has confirmed that the footage in question will be removed from the game through an update slated for release on November 12th.

In real life, the crush from which the footage was taken took place in the Itaewon district during Halloween. With more than 100,000 people in the area, many found themselves packed together tightly enough that many were unable to breathe. Family members of those affected by the disaster called it “a tragedy that caused hundreds of casualties, and citizens who took part in the rescue or witnessed it have suffered from trauma until now”.

Further in their statements, the families said, “We cannot help but be appalled that the chaotic and desperate scene, in which many citizens at the site performed CPR to save one another, and victims lay on the freezing street, was used to create a scene in a game.

“Using such an event as material for a game made for entertainment is in itself highly inappropriate and senseless.”

In its own statement, Activision has said that the depiction of the Seoul crush was intended “to establish the game’s fictional narrative” and that the company had “not intended to reference or trivialize the Itaewon disaster or any other real-world tragedy in any way, shape or form.” In a statement to the BBC, the 10.29 Itaewon Disaster Bereaved Families Association praised Activision’s decision to remove the footage.

This is not the first time that a game in the Modern Warfare sub-franchise has found itself in the midst of this kind of controversy. 2009’s Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 2 became the focus of much discussion and criticism due to a noteworthy level dubbed No Russian, which puts players in the shoes of a soldier who is also undercover in a terrorist organization and directly participates in a mass shootout in a crowded airport.

Similarly, the 2019 reboot of Modern Warfare was criticized for its depiction of the “Highway of Death”, which in the game is set in the fictional country of Urzikstan, but was based on the real-world Highway 80 – the road between Kuwait and Basra that was heavily bombed by the US military and its allies during the 1991 Gulf War.

The Legend of Zelda Series Producer is Worried About Ocarina of Time Remake Ahead of Release

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Despite The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time being widely considered to be one of the greatest games of all time, series producer Eiji Aonuma has admitted that he is still quite worried about the release of the upcoming remake. In an interview with AFP (via Japan Today), Aonuma spoke about the title’s release.

“I’m all nervous about releasing the remake of ‘Ocarina’ because I don’t know how it’s going to turn out; I can’t stop thinking about it,” he said. While many might find Aonuma’s thoughts on the matter puzzling, his worries shouldn’t come as a surprise to anyone since the upcoming title is a remake of a game that was originally released all the way back in 1998.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time has always been well-regarded for defining many of the aspects that would go on to become core parts of the entire franchise’s identity. This ranged from the Z-targeting lock-on system to the interplay between unique items and dungeons, puzzle design, and even how the core combat works. Not only have these aspects been further refined in the almost three decades since the original release, but more fans who got into the Zelda franchise in more recent times might even find them puzzling.

The last mainline entries in the series have been The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild and The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom. Both of these titles had an emphasis on the open world, player traversal, and a gameplay sandbox that could allow players to toy around with the special tools given to them early in the games. However, Ocarina of Time, while still featuring a relatively open design, is a lot more linear than what modern fans might be used to.

Interestingly, the fact that the Ocarina of Time remake might bring in a younger audience plays a major role in Aonuma’s worries. “It’s thanks to these young people, by thinking about how to adapt the game in the best possible way, how to make it fun once you have the controls in your hands, that this remake came into being.”

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time’s remake was officially unveiled in June with a trailer giving us our first look at the game’s depiction of Hyrule in modern visual fidelity. The title is also set to include a few other changes over the original, such as the addition of a dedicated jump button, expansions to various zones like Kakariko Village and Goron City, a streamlined inventory system that allows for single-button item swapping, and even some entirely new additions, like boss rematches and the Hall of Fallen Foes.

The remake is making its way to the Nintendo Switch 2 on November 5th. The title faces quite a bit of stiff competition, since it is coming out just two weeks before the juggernaut that is Rockstar’s Grand Theft Auto 6.
