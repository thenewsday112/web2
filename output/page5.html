Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls Details Phoenix Cyclops’s Skills and Alternate Form in New Video

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

With Phoenix Cyclops joining the roster of Marvel Tōkon: Fighting Souls as its first DLC character, Arc System Works has released a new Character Guide video, detailing his abilities. Check it out below.

It begins with Optic Blast, a simple beam attack that can be charged for more damage, bounce off the floor, and even evade projectiles. This is also one way to gain Solar Shards and trigger Dark Phoenix form, enhancing attacks with Surging Flame, letting you move in a specific direction after it connects (and takes some health). So when using other skills like Heat Wave, Tactical Strike, and Phoenix Dive, it opens up a lot of potential for mix-ups.

However, you need to decide carefully when to use it because Solar Shards can’t be garnered for the rest of the round after the transformation ends. Afterwards, Cyclops enters the Ashen State, possessing many of the same abilities as his default state.

As for his Ultimate Skill, Dark Ascension, it can change depending on these conditions. If used after collecting all the Solar Shards, he’ll transform into Dark Phoenix; without them, he’ll immediately attack and transition into the Ashen state. Use it when he’s already Dark Phoenix, and you’ll deal far more damage.

Phoenix Cyclops arrives on November 2nd for Year 1 Character and Stage Pass holders. He’s followed by Thor (Jane Foster), Venom and Jubilee.

The Avatar State Patch is available today for Avatar Legends: The Fighting Game on XBOX Series X|S, and it’s the biggest update the game has seen. It brings a fresh new look for the game’s menus and in-match displays, two totally reimagined fighters, Ranked Mode 2.0, quality-of-life upgrades for story, versus, and online play, plus balance changes for every character on the roster.

Since the game launched earlier this year with 12 characters from “Avatar: The Last Airbender” and “The Legend of Korra,” fighters have been putting their bending (or boomerangs) to the test, and our team has been watching closely. This patch is the result. It’s the game we envisioned from the beginning, and now it’s ready for you to play.

What’s in the Avatar State Patch:

  • A new look, everywhere. Menus, character select, stage select and the in-match interface have all been redesigned.
  • Two reimagined fighters. Avatar Aang and Nightmare Korra return with new art, new attacks and new super moves.
  • Every fighter rebalanced. From Aang to Zaheer, every character has been fine-tuned for fairer, more exciting matches.
  • More ways to play and learn. Story Mode+, Story difficulty settings, new tutorials and Combo Trials, Story stages in Versus, and more.

Reimagined Characters: Avatar Aang and Nightmare Korra

The two fighters at the heart of this patch are among the most powerful forms in the Avatar universe. Studio Art Director Giancarlo Montalbano, Art Director Santiago R. Villa, and Combat Director Young share how they brought them to life, and why they took the pair in opposite directions. “For Aang, his Avatar pose looks to elevate his presence in all ways, while for Korra it looks to diminish it. One is solemn and imposing while the other is languid and elusive.” Villa explains.

Avatar Aang

Avatar Aang’s character sheet, with his glowing Air Nomad tattoos idle pose, full turnaround and the lightning scar on his back.

In the Avatar State, Aang taps into the power of every Avatar who came before him. Young points to his final showdown with Fire Lord Ozai as the biggest touchstone, along with Avatar State moments across the series, from channeling Kyoshi on Avatar Day to facing the Sandbenders. While regular Aang is playful, Young describes this version as pure, overwhelming power, landing precise elemental attacks with the smallest movements. Villa adds that he’s the only fighter in the game lit from below, a deliberate choice to make him look mysterious and boss-like. Young’s favorite move is his signature special, Avatar’s Ascension, which lifts him into an air sphere packed with callbacks to the Ozai fight.

His new Level 4 super, his most powerful attack, brings in a special guest: Avatar Roku, straight out of the Winter Solstice two-parter. “Aang’s direct manifestations of prior Avatars ended up being something very unique to him,” says Young, “so we felt like we had to include it!”

Montalbano admits Avatar Aang was the trickier of the two, since his arm tattoos had to read correctly from every angle. Villa shares another hard call: Aang’s foot scar is missing, because with fighters switching sides, it would have been on the wrong foot half the time.

Nightmare Korra

Nightmare Korra’s character sheet. Her hunched idle pose, chains on both wrists, and menacingly glowing eyes set her apart from Korra.

Young explains that Nightmare Korra is the dark reflection that haunted Korra in “The Legend of Korra” episode Korra Alone, with her final fight against Zaheer at the end of Book 3 as another influence. “She’s a manifestation of that berserk state…alongside being a kind of spooky, ghost-like entity so we wanted to try and hit both of those angles pretty sharply. So you’ll see big contrasts between her slowly dragging her feet, then suddenly exploding into big chain swings or teleports.” Villa notes that she now wears chains on both wrists instead of one, a nod to how much weight they carry in her story.

Her visual effects are some of the wildest in the game. Montalbano highlights the mercury in her super moves and a victory pose that zooms through a fish-eye lens into her transformation into Vaatu, which he calls “very unique compared to a lot of other stuff in the game.” For Young, the standout is her Final Technique, Eternal Nightmare, a big team effort that looks just as striking when it misses as when it lands.

Young also shared a fun fact: early prototypes had her health constantly draining to represent mercury poisoning. It didn’t make for interesting play, so she simply starts with less health instead.

What’s new in battle

Battle Balance

There’s a lot to dig into, but here’s a closer look at two of the biggest shifts.

Azula’s Ambition’s Spark, her Focused Stance exclusive move, has vastly improved to allow much more variety in gameplay. It now automatically tracks much closer to your opponent’s position. Its detonation is a much greater hitbox and can also be flow cancelable.

Sokka’s sword special techniques, under certain circumstances using his Master Piandao support, can now directly cancel into a boomerang toss! Upon having at least 10 stacks of successful sword hits, Sokka permanently unlocks that ability for the rest of the match!

You can find the full patch notes on the Paramount Games site when the patch goes live.

Nightmare Korra: a new moveset to match

Nightmare Korra’s new look goes deeper than just that. Her kit has been rebuilt to match her haunting presence.

Avatar Aang: enter Roku!

Roku’s Inferno is Avatar Aang’s new Super Technique. Aang communicates with the spirit world, temporarily transforming him into Roku who engulfs the entire battlefield with a scorching flame.

  • Chaotic new take on hockey out now on Xbox Series X|S.
  • Brutal fights, ridiculous takedowns and riveting action within a unique narrative.
  • How we imagined a game inspired by childhood hockey playing and storytelling.

What happens when five Finnish developers take the hockey they grew up playing on frozen ponds and turn it into a story about the greatest underground hockey player who ever lived?

When I think about my childhood, some of my best memories aren’t from school, holidays or birthday parties.

They’re from the ice.

Growing up in Finland, I spent enormous amounts of time on frozen outdoor rinks and ponds, playing hockey with friends. You didn’t need to organize it weeks in advance or check everyone’s calendar. You just went to the rink. Games happened. Friendships happened. Some of the people I met through hockey back then are still my friends today.

There was something special about those games. The rules could be flexible, teams could change, and somebody would inevitably try something ridiculous. That feeling became one of the starting points for Puckoff.

When we created Puckoff: Reloaded, we wanted to bring some of that feeling back. Many of the game’s rinks are frozen outdoor ponds inspired by the places where we grew up playing.

Then we asked ourselves a simple question: What if winning didn’t mean scoring more goals?

A Different Kind of Hockey

In most hockey games, you know what you’re supposed to do: Get the puck. Score goals. Win the game. In Puckoff: Reloaded, there are a few more options. You can score. Set up a teammate. Make the perfect pass. Or you can knock someone out.

And nobody in this world seems particularly bothered by that last option.

That’s the world we wanted to create: a story-driven hockey game where fighting isn’t a random side activity, but part of the sport and its strange underground culture. I’ve played a lot of the big hockey games over the years. They’re great at what they do, but I always found myself thinking: “Why can’t I just do that?”

You know the feeling. An opponent does something you don’t like, and you want to give them a little… extra attention. But the game tells you no. That’s when I realized one of the things I love about games. They let us experiment, cause chaos and do things we wouldn’t necessarily do in real life. So, I thought: what if a hockey game actually embraced that? That’s how Puckoff: Reloaded began.

In our version of hockey, scoring goals is still useful. It’s just not the only way to win.

We’re Not Just Making a Hockey Game

I’ve always loved storytelling, and for years I’ve had a particular dream: What would it be like if a big hockey game actually had a real story? Not just a career mode where you play a season and upgrade your player. I mean a proper story. Characters you care about. Mysteries. Rivalries. Friendships. A reason to wonder what happens next. That’s what we wanted Puckoff: Reloaded to be.

Between matches, the game uses a visual-novel-inspired presentation to tell a larger story. The hockey games aren’t disconnected challenges. They’re part of a journey. And that journey is about finding a legend.

The Search for Dominator

At the heart of Puckoff: Reloaded is a hockey legend named Dominator. He’s been playing underground hockey matches for decades, and nobody really knows how he’s managed to keep going for so long. What everyone does know is that he has never lost a game.

For the player character, Dominator isn’t just another hockey player. He’s the reason to keep playing. The dream is simple: find Dominator and eventually play alongside him. Getting there means meeting the people who inhabit this underground hockey world and proving you’re good enough to belong in it. Eventually, that leads to the Cage.

Welcome to the Cage

The biggest fights in Puckoff: Reloaded don’t happen in a traditional hockey arena. They happen underground.

The Cage is where some of the game’s most dangerous opponents are waiting. These boss matches are different from ordinary games: you’re not simply facing another team and trying to score more goals. You’re facing a character with their own identity, abilities and way of playing. And if you’re going to get closer to Dominator, you’ll have to get through them.

Our Dream is Bigger Than This Game

I’m proud of what our five-person team has created, but I also see Puckoff: Reloaded as the beginning of something. My dream has always been to make story-driven hockey games with the scope and production values of the big-budget games. I want hockey games where you remember the characters as much as the matches. Stories that make you care who wins. I want a game where you can spend an evening playing hockey with your friends, laughing when somebody gets knocked out—and then come back the next day because you genuinely want to know what happens to the characters.

We’re not a huge studio. We’re five people in Finland making the game we wanted to play. For me, that’s what Puckoff has always been about. It started with the frozen ponds where some of my best childhood memories were made. Now we’re taking those memories, adding a ridiculous amount of fighting, creating a hockey legend who hasn’t lost in decades, and turning the whole thing into a story.

We just have to find Dominator first.

What GTA Clones Did Better Than Grand Theft Auto Itself

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Grand Theft Auto represents the pinnacle of AAA games, not just in huge-scale production values, but in the kind of innovation that rewrites the rules for what games can even be. It seems only Rockstar is capable of fully nailing this style of game. Of course, with its level of success, that hasn't stopped other developers from trying.

Rightly or wrongly, these games are often dubbed GTA clones, a label that automatically assumes it's an inferior imitation to the real McCoy. However, that doesn't mean these games don't have their redeeming features and innovations that offer something different, or in some cases, even arguably improves on GTA's shortcomings--yes, even one of the greatest game series of all time is imperfect.

Even as GTA 6 promises to be the most refined vision of open-world gaming that eclipses all the competition, there's still a good chance you'll find some feature in these clones that’s more compelling than Rockstar's own criminal paradise.

Mafia (2002)

Mafia (Definitive Edition)

"Open-world game" for many players means having the freedom to explore and do whatever you want within a large sandbox. However, Mafia's Lost Heaven was very much a "faux-pen world" that served as one large movie set you'd just drive around from mission to mission. While that might sound like a criticism, it worked in favor for Czech developer Illusion Softworks and its atmospheric period piece set in 1930s America.

In taking a more mature and serious direction, Mafia was also more realistic in many ways, from the real-time damage of vehicles to a more in-depth minor- and serious-offense system from the cops compared to GTA's star-based Wanted System. It may not appeal to players looking for a sandbox for wanton criminal chaos, but when it comes to telling the story of a humble man's rise and fall in the world of crime, and the ultimate consequences, few games do it as memorably.

Scarface: The World Is Yours (2006)

Scarface: The World is Yours

GTA clones may imitate GTA, but you can also argue that GTA very consciously pastiches popular crime and gangster dramas, such as Vice City's amalgamation of Miami Vice and Scarface. So you can certainly see the appeal of a game where you get to play Tony Montana for real, based on Al Pacino's likeness, while André Sogliuzzo (handpicked by Pacino himself) makes for a faithful soundalike.

While licensed games can get a bad rap for being lazy cash-ins, Scarface: The World Is Yours is a great example of not just aping the source material but expanding it with an alt-universe scenario where Montana survives the original 1983 movie's finale and then mounts a campaign of revenge. More importantly, it introduced ideas and mechanics that improved upon the other GTAs at the time, from a more robust targeting system to a deep money-laundering system that also impacted the heat you'd get from cops and rival gangs.

Most delicious of all is a Balls meter that builds up when performing a range of actions like taunting enemies and driving dangerously, which then activates a ridiculously OP rage mode, preceding a similar mechanic from GTA 5's Trevor years later.

Saint's Row 2 (2008)

Saints Row 2

When you think "GTA clone," Saint's Row is probably the one that springs to mind, being the first to take Rockstar's crime-filled open-world formula to the HD era. But it's really with its sequel where it carves out its own identity, opting for a more over-the-top and zany tone that wasn't trying to be realistic, which was all the more notable at the time when GTA 4 went down the path of subdued, gritty realism.

Volition's series excels in giving the player the freedom to fully create and customize their own protagonist. That in turn gives you the option to create a "Boss" that you can actually have fun spending time with in the open world of Stilwater for many hours compared to GTA's prescribed sociopaths. And when this genre often carries the baggage of being testosterone-fueled, being able to create a fully voiced female protagonist who can go on crime sprees in 2008 was a ballsy move when it's taken nearly two decades for Rockstar to give us its first playable heroine.

Sleeping Dogs (2012)

Sleeping Dogs (Definitive Edition)

My personal pick for GTA clone that I would happily  replay over any GTA has to be Sleeping Dogs. It’s already refreshing by transporting the urban open world template to Hong Kong, which while not a geographically accurate representation of the former British colony, nonetheless nails its atmosphere from the background chatter of local Cantonese voices to the humid and neon-lit streets.

But where Sleeping Dogs excels over GTA is its combat. That's not just in the martial arts-inspired melee brawling, which features some truly brutal takedown animations, but even in the gunplay where you can vault over cover and then enter bullet-time like you're in your own John Woo action movie. While there's criticism over its arcadey driving mechanics, I'm willing to forgive it when you can jump out of your speeding car to hijack someone else's.

Yakuza 5 (2012)

Yakuza 5

Apart from its mature gangster themes, the Yakuza games really aren't like GTA at all, though that doesn't stop people from making the comparison. But where GTA offers huge open sandboxes, Yakuza excels in its density. With locations like Kamurocho (based on Tokyo's infamous red light district Kabukicho) it really does feel like you're exploring its streets intimately, where there are plenty of shops and businesses you can enter instead of walking past a lot of glorified set-dressing.

With so many entries to choose from, it would be easy to pick a newer installment with more robust visuals and a first-person exploration option that allows you to better appreciate the details inside a Japanese convenience store. But Yakuza 5 is an apt choice here because it's one of the only entries that includes driving sections, during which you’re driving a taxi that has to follow traffic rules properly one moment and then speed down a highway in an arcade racer against a street-racing gang the next.

That's only a fraction of the different minigames you can enjoy. Whether it's job simulation like running a ramen stand, or teenager Haruka performing J-pop routines via rhythm action games, or relaxing with bowling, or even playing Virtua Fighter 2 in the arcade, there's just so many things to do. And it's all executed with compelling arcade audio-visual feedback that Sega has naturally accrued decades of experience in.

Lego City Undercover (2013)

Lego City Undercover

GTA may be for mature audiences but Lego City Undercover asks, "What if you could take that popular open-world game design and make it family-friendly?" Obviously, that requires some significant adjustments, such as how your protagonist Chase McClain is working for the law on the hunt for an escaped convict, while its Lego City is more accurately an overworld made up of smaller levels or districts.

Nonetheless, when you can explore a bustling city with dozens of vehicles or also have some playful on-foot traversal like wall-jumping and swinging from poles, while constantly hearing plenty of gags riffing on pop culture, it's an age-appropriate entry point to Rockstar's style of open-world games ... even if you have to admit that breaking the law is a lot more fun.

Watch Dogs 2 (2016)

Watch Dogs 2

Ubisoft may already specialize in open-world games like Assassin's Creed and Far Cry, but Watch Dogs' contemporary urban setting puts it firmly in the category of GTA clone, though its hacking mechanics also set it apart. But while the first game's moody setting, story, and protagonist made for an offputting debut, Watch Dogs 2 doesn't just improve on its predecessor--some would even argue it's a more fun time than GTA.

Marcus Holloway makes for a cooler and more relatable protagonist, and while San Francisco's map isn't on the same scale as GTA 5's Los Santos, it's often a lot livelier given your ability to hack just about everything or interact with the city's huge cast of dynamic NPCs, often leading to unpredictable but memorable situations.

Phantom Blade Zero

Wesley LeBlanc

Phantom Blade Zero is one of the best action games I’ve ever played, and it’s certainly one of the most impressive to watch. Beijing-based developer S-Game uses China’s Wuxia brand of action (which was actually banned for some time during the 20th Century by the country’s government) to create a stylish, fluid, and hyper-engaging combat system. It will constantly leave you in awe that action combat can look this good. But looks aren’t everything. Fortunately, Phantom Blade Zero delivers on all fronts, with weapon mechanics so detailed that picking up a new blade can feel like unlocking a new character in a fighting game. The story won’t blow your socks off, but it’s certainly engaging and enticing enough to bring you through Phantom Blade Zero’s 40 or so hours of content. If you’re not interested in the “perfect ending,” which is the only satisfactory one in my opinion, you can complete the game in less than 30 hours. S-Game mixes high-octane combat with sprawling and rewarding exploration to create a must-play during this already busy season of games. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Phantom Blade Zero review, and if you are hopping in this weekend, be sure to check out my list of Phantom Blade Zero tips and tricks I wish I knew before starting it.

Halloween: The Game

Wesley LeBlanc

If you have any fond memories of IllFonic’s ill-fated Friday the 13th: The Game as I do, you simply must play the studio’s latest, Halloween: The Game. It is essentially Friday the 13th with Michael Myers instead of Jason, but with a decade of lessons learned from the team’s teeth carving in other horror properties and games. You’re either Michael or a citizen; the former wants to kill you and all the NPCs in various suburban maps, while the latter must rescue as many NPCs as possible while staying alive themselves. It’s that classic cat-and-mouse formula IllFonic is the master of, but with all the style, substance, and synths of John Carpenter’s Halloween masterpiece. Plus, Halloween: The Game features IllFonic’s first crack at a single-player campaign, and while it’s largely a tutorial for playing as Michael, if you’re a Halloween mega-fan like me, it’s worth checking out if only to take part in some of the film’s most iconic kills. You can read more of my thoughts in the Game Informer Halloween: The Game review here, and if you’re hopping in this weekend, there’s a new map, additional Halloween-themed cosmetics to unlock, and more waiting for you.

Tom The Postgirl

Charles Harte

Tom the Postgirl is an odd little adventure that I'd recommend to fans of Untitled Goose Game. You play as Tom, a creepy girl who's tasked with delivering packages to the people of her wacky town, which is depicted with storybook-style illustrations and a color palette limited to black, white, and red. On the surface, you're delivering packages, but the game's real story is based on Tom's tendency to peep where she shouldn't, peering into townsfolks' windows to watch their odd lives. On house is full of kids who live like dogs; another has a couple with a bizarre obsession with tickling. You never know what each window might reveal, and I quickly sympathized with (and enabled) Tom's compulsion. You also have the capacity to cause quite a bit of trouble by stomping on flowers, haphazardly snipping strings and cords, or even stealing packages and using them for your own mischievous purposes. It's a pretty short experience, completable in just a few hours, making it a nice palate cleanser between the massive releases at the end of the year.

Virtue And A Sledgehammer

Marcus Stewart

This Devolver Digital-published game has a simple but head-turning premise. You’re a profoundly angry woman who returns to her small hometown armed with nothing but a sledgehammer, which she uses to smash everything in sight. The town is also inhabited by robots possessing human consciousnesses. Swinging the hammer can either punch holes through walls or, with enough force, reduce entire buildings to rubble. But why is she doing this? Why is she so mad? A big part of Virtue and a Sledgehammer’s allure is its fascinating and well-written tale about the protagonist’s strained relationships with her sister and mother. Learning more about their lives requires you to keep smashing, but the more you learn, the more you begin to question whether smashing everything is actually a good idea.

Titanium Court On Switch 2

Marcus Stewart

This bizarre indie blends fun match-3 puzzle-solving with tower defense gameplay wrapped around a funny but surreal story. The match-3 gameplay involves sliding tiles to match geographic elements like castles, trees, and rivers to collect resources that feed into the tower defense portion. Depending on how the board looks when the puzzle portion ends, you send troops (sustained by the resources you collected) to attack/defend against rival castles and gather more items; board positioning determines how well this can go. The two mechanics feed into each other to create a fun loop that grows in complexity as new mechanics are introduced. Did I mention the game is also very weird? The silly tale centers on a medieval court designating you the chosen savior to rescue their lost queen (and many other tasks), and the dumb tone adds personality to a sometimes dry genre. Titanium Court charmed and impressed us when it launched on PC earlier this year, and now console and mobile players get their chance to hold court.

Minecraft On Switch 2

Charles Harte

Minecraft has held its dominant spot in pop culture for so long that it's easy to forget a simple fact about it: the game is awesome. Tabletop Pick – Arkham Horror: The Card Game

Matt Miller

The card game incarnation of Arkham Horror remains one of the best narrative-driven and horror-themed tabletop games around, and it's an easy recommendation if you're looking for something engaging to dive into on Halloween night. Rooted in the monsters and mysteries of the Cthulhu Mythos, this living card game is built on major core sets and expansions. But unlike a TCG, each new installment has the same cards included, fueling the cooperative adventures you'll undertake together with your fellow players. Last year Arkham Horror: The Card Game did something of a soft relaunch for a chapter 2 of its narrative, and that new core set is an ideal place to jump on  play. Players each control an individual investigator as they explore arcane and weird mysteries, confront monsters, and unfold plots of cultists, ancient entities, and forbidden lore. For fans of cosmic horror storytelling, it's an easy pick.

Curse of Resthaven: Tips and Tricks to Unravel the Island’s Secrets

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hi there! I’m Scott Christian, the studio director of Hilltop Studios here in Toronto, and the game director of Curse of Resthaven. We make games with a particular emphasis on strong characters, intricate storytelling, unique mechanics, and the occasional musical interlude.

Before making games, I spent fifteen years working as a composer and conductor here in Toronto, mainly in theatre. That background informs how we approach our games at Hilltop: character, timing, and a good dramatic reveal go a long way. Our debut, Lil’ Guardsman, was a comedic fantasy gate-guard simulator about a twelve-year-old put in charge of a castle gate, with the fate of the world in her hands.

For our follow-up, Curse of Resthaven, we decided to take a different turn into gothic horror, creeping dread, and a mystery that unfolds over a repeating six-day time loop. You play as Ambrose, the island’s newly appointed governor, tasked with restoring prosperity to the colony but he also has his own agenda: finding his missing niece, Amalia. The problem is that Resthaven is destroyed six days into his tenure… and Ambrose is sent back to the day of his arrival, the only one to remember the island’s fate.

Mashing up mechanics from narrative adventures, trading sims, deckbuilders, and roguelikes, Curse of Resthaven challenges you to investigate a town full of secrets, keep its economy running, and venture into the strange wilderness beyond its borders. You have six days to work with before disaster strikes and the island resets. Fortunately, you get to keep what you learn.

Solving the mystery is just the first step. You’ll soon discover there are three different timelines to explore, and only by reaching all three conclusions will you unlock the fourth, true ending.

Whether you’re stepping onto Resthaven for the first time or looking to make the most of your next loop, here are a few tips to help you get started.

A Few Simple Tips

Curse of Resthaven is a game about connecting the dots. Its systems feed into one another, and the best discoveries often come from combining information gathered in different ways. Here are a few things to keep in mind as you investigate the island.

  • Talk to everyone, and then talk to them again. Resthaven’s residents have their own motivations, grudges, and secrets. What someone tells you depends on what Ambrose already knows, what topics you bring up, and what has happened during the current loop. If a character isn’t being helpful, you may simply need to learn a little more before approaching them. Your journal can be counted on to let you know when to ask someone about something again.
  • Pay attention during interrogations. You can question characters about different topics to uncover clues, advance quests, and learn more about the island. Information gathered from one resident may unlock the topic needed to move the needle with another. If you’re stuck, revisit people you’ve spoken to and see whether they have a perspective on newly acquired topics or, better yet, can guide you in the right direction.
  • Don’t treat trading as a side activity. As governor, Ambrose is responsible for keeping Resthaven’s economy moving. Merchant ships bring goods to buy and sell, while the island’s businesses can be upgraded to produce useful resources. Money is only part of the equation: having the right resources is essential for pursuing opportunities elsewhere, including expeditions into the wilderness.
  • Experiment with your approach. Not every problem has an obvious solution, and not every promising lead will pay off immediately. Try different approaches, pursue unexpected conversations, and see where your investigations take you. Even a botched loop may teach you something useful for the next one.

Make the Most of the Time Loop

The six-day time loop is at the heart of Curse of Resthaven. Each loop begins with the island’s inhabitants returning to their usual routines, but Ambrose retains his knowledge and progression. That distinction is everything.

You aren’t expected to solve the entire mystery in one pass. Instead, each loop gives you another opportunity to apply what you’ve learned, approach old problems differently, and uncover information that was previously out of reach.

  • Knowledge is your most valuable resource. A character who refuses to cooperate on Day 1 might be much more forthcoming once you’ve discovered what motivates them. An apparently impossible obstacle might become straightforward once you know the right person to talk to or the right question to ask.
  • Plan your days around what you want to learn. There are more people to question, businesses to manage, and places to explore than you can necessarily get to in a single loop. Focus on the leads that seem most promising, and don’t worry if you have to leave something for next time.
  • Use each reset as an opportunity. When the island resets, you can take a different approach with the benefit of everything you’ve discovered. You don’t have to repeat the same actions just because you did them last time. If you already know where a particular lead is going, spend that time pursuing something new.

The goal isn’t simply to repeat the same six days until you get everything right. It’s to use what you learned last time to change what happens the next time.

Venture Beyond the Town

Beyond the safety of Resthaven’s fortified walls lies a much stranger version of the island. Expeditions take Ambrose into this wilderness through a card-based narrative system that blends roguelikes, deckbuilders, and choose-your-own-adventure games.

Each expedition draws encounters from a pool, meaning your journey can unfold differently from one attempt to the next. You’ll face decisions, discover strange events, and encounter opportunities that may not appear again on your next trip.

  • Expect the unexpected. Expeditions aren’t fixed sequences of events. The encounters you discover on one outing may be very different from those on another, so stay curious and be prepared to adapt.
  • Don’t assume you’ve seen everything. There are more encounters than you’ll experience in a single run. Returning to the wilderness on subsequent playthroughs can reveal entirely new stories and discoveries.
  • Remember that expeditions are part of the larger investigation. The wilderness isn’t separate from the mystery unfolding in town. Information and resources you gather can help you make progress elsewhere, so consider how an expedition might support your other goals.

Explore Every Corner Of Resthaven

It’s worth taking the time to look around. Beyond the main investigation, Resthaven contains optional discoveries, including collectible moth relics and other secrets tucked away throughout the town.

These discoveries reward players who aren’t in a rush. You can barely take a single step if you rely heavily on the handy town map + fast travel functionality, but you’ll certainly miss out on some optional, flavourful side content. Give yourself permission to wander a little. After all, in Resthaven you have nothing but time (in six-day intervals)!

And don’t be afraid to revisit familiar places. As you learn more about the colony and its inhabitants, locations and conversations that seemed unremarkable at first may take on new significance.

What To Do If You’re Stuck

Your journal is your best friend. Check your active leads for hints about what to pursue next. If you’ve exhausted all the possibilities in town, it may be time to head back out on expeditions and look for Amalia’s campsites. And your journal will probably tell you so!

Your journal has another trick up its sleeve, too. After the opening hours, you’ll unlock a timeline showing important events across the six-day loop and where they take place. If you’re stumped by a “???” on the timeline, it means something important is happening at that moment you haven’t discovered yet. To progress, you might just need to be in the right place at the right time.

The town map is another easy-to-miss resource. It shows which characters are accessible in each zone at any given time. Once you’ve spoken to a named NPC, their question mark becomes a portrait, making it much easier to track them down in subsequent loops. Still seeing a “?” in a zone? That might be a good place to start looking for someone new.

Finally, don’t underestimate the power of a little community gossip. Some leads require interrogation, but the answer might simply be to ask the townspeople about a particular topic. Resthaven is a small, tight-knit community, and information has a way of getting around.

We’ve designed the investigation around a trail of clues(We’ve classed them as  bronze/silver/gold behind the scenes, but you won’t see these terms in-game.)

  • Bronze clues give you a little information and nudge you in the right direction, and many townspeople have them
  • Silver clues offer more specific details and bring you closer to the truth; fewer townspeople have them
  • Gold clues unambiguously reveal the next step in a lead. Only one or two townspeople have them

Follow the trail, and you shouldn’t be left out in the cold for long.

Don’t Worry About Seeing Everything the First Time

Curse of Resthaven features three branching timelines, each revealing different pieces of the larger mystery. You won’t necessarily encounter every quest or revelation during a single playthrough, and that’s intentional.

As you progress, you’ll uncover new information that changes how you understand the island and its people. Some discoveries will answer questions, while others will leave you with even more to investigate.

Once you’ve completed a timeline, New Game+ introduces Ambrose’s growing sense of déjà vu, allowing you to fast-track through content you’ve already experienced and focus on what’s different. You can spend less time retracing familiar steps and more time exploring new possibilities.

So don’t worry about finding every secret on your first visit. Pay attention, follow the leads that interest you, and let your growing knowledge guide you through the next loop.

Resthaven has a great many secrets. The trick is figuring out which ones to pursue, when to pursue them, and what to do with what you discover.

Six days remain. Good luck, Governor. You’ll need it.
