Ramayana: Rise of a Legend Review

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ramayana: Rise of a Legend is in theaters beginning Nov. 5.

Hindi cinema has long looked to Hollywood as a benchmark for big-screen action; much of its 21st century style, for instance, was inspired by Mission: Impossible 2. Ramayana: Rise of a Legend re-imagines the resplendent Hindu epic as a CGI-laden blockbuster at a time when the Marvel Cinematic Universe has become the de facto touchstone for big-budget adaptations. In that vein, Nitesh Tiwari’s undertaking does, in fact, resemble a Marvel movie. Unfortunately, that Marvel movie happens to be Thor: Love and Thunder, a much-derided film whose floating head VFX had to be famously reworked.

This resemblance takes hold in an early scene, in which the epic’s antagonist, Ravana (charismatic Toxic star Yash), prays atop a cliff to the creator-god Brahma, whose countenance takes the form of a disembodied constellation with an overly-expressive digital mouth. This excess fluidity is owed, in part, to the movie’s uncanny English dub, which was shown to press in the United States in lieu of the original Hindi version (which happens to be 18 minutes longer than this 162-minute cut). Rather than traditional dubbing, the filmmakers developed their own in-house Generative A.I. technology (fittingly called Brahma A.I.) to sync up the characters’ lips to the flowery English dialogue. Some are calling it the future of cinema, but in its current incarnation, the result resembles exaggerated mouth movements superimposed on still images for comedy sketches, à la The Annoying Orange.

This effect is befitting of such a robotic adaptation, one which briefly threatens to interrogate the several millennia-old text of the title (in the vein of recent Thai drama The Burning Giants, which critiques the violence at the heart of national myths) before locking itself into a didactic approach. It’s a rigid depiction of both the subcontinent-spanning plot, as well as the theme of “Dharma,” or sacred duty, which the characters verbalize ad nauseum and follow unconditionally, and uninspiringly. For the uninitiated — which is to say, Sony’s target audience for the English dub, whose Indian subtitle (“Part 1”) has been slyly replaced with “Rise of a Legend” to convince people they’re paying for a complete story — the plot concerns an ongoing war between demigods the Devas and demons the Asuras.

The film re-imagines the villainous Ravana as a kind of liberator gone mad. The Asura leader begins by praying for the power to free his enslaved clan, and his island kingdom of Lanka, from Deva oppression before conquering nearby cities through violent conquest with his ambitious siblings by his side.. This forces the demigods to turn to the protector deity Vishnu, who re-incarnates himself as the human-born Rama (Ranbir Kapoor), one of four princes of the kingdom of Ayodhya. Prologues pile up on prologues for nearly an hour, as Rama grows up learning the ways of war and wisdom, since his divine destiny is to defeat the Asuras for good.

The filmmakers’ decision to cleave the story in half leaves it hanging in suspended animation.

However, the filmmakers’ decision to cleave the story in half leaves it hanging in suspended animation. It ends where the central plot of the Ramayana — Rama’s journey to rescuing his devoted wife Sita (Sai Pallavi) from Ravana’s clutches — finally kicks off in earnest, yielding an episodic experience where the central hero and villain are, at best, vaguely aware of one another’s existence. They never actually come into contact, leaving Ramayana: Rise of a Legend with the responsibility of establishing a protagonist and antagonist alluring enough that viewers with no pre-existing religious or cultural attachment will feel dutybound to return in one year’s time — or whenever the sequel is ready.

How does this work out? As far as Ravana is concerned, Kannada-language superstar Yash makes a meal of the character, imagined as an action anti-hero who dances in fiery worship of the destroyer god Shiva, and who nails his enemies with jackknife powerbombs, as vengeful gears turn slowly in his mind. Traditionally depicted with 10 heads side by side, the movie’s version of Ravana re-imagines this as a kind of fissure of personality, wherein 10 different versions of the demon king (some old, some young; some confident, some sniveling) converse and argue within his consciousness. He’s ruthless in one moment, but respectful in the next, making for the closest thing the movie has to complexity before it returns to the story’s binary good-versus-evil traditions. Add to this the fact that the movie’s Lanka is vibrant, energetic and sensual, compared to its stilted, austere Ayodhya, and it becomes hard to root for the ostensible “good guys.”

Unfortunately, this becomes doubly true whenever Rama is on screen. The film’s depiction of him is an utter bore. An embodiment of virtue in the abstract, the heroic prince — who agrees to live in exile for 14 years so one of his brothers can take the throne — is spoken of as a kind and capable figure, but his people’s worship of him is a default setting, rather than something earned through benevolence. After all, Rama is a central figure in Hinduism, so more than enough viewers will bring their own baggage to the movie. But as a story, it’s as rote as can be, a flaw that infects its dramatic DNA: Rama’s relationship to Sita. While Pallavi is afforded a tad more action and autonomy than most actresses in the role, the promise of a sword-wielding Sita is often cut short, returning her quickly to a two-dimensional damsel who shares little chemistry with her husband. Much like Kapoor’s Rama, Pallavi’s Sita is graceful in posture, but there’s little underlying either of them as actual human beings.

Rama, who lacks anything resembling outward opinions or an inner life beyond his Dharma, acquires weapons and knowledge not through practice or learning, but through gurus psychically unlocking these hidden abilities, as though the film were operating on video game logic. Each new chapter sees him “leveling up” as he fights a different, malformed CGI golem in a new forest setting, though at least he deserves credit for using his unlimited magical arrows to get these eyesores off our screens.

Saddled with clunky English dialogue, Kapoor is the perfect actor for this version of Rama, but that’s not the compliment it seems. His po-faced approach snaps perfectly into place, alongside a dramatically dull incarnation of the mythic hero, whose duty toward violent reprisal, and whose adoration by his legions of fans, is a given. Kapoor, a fourth generation Bollywood star, enjoys exactly such a quasi-religious fandom, even if the Hindu nationalist factions the film has been courting with its marketing (which refers to these myths as “history”) has since turned on him for eating meat — a religious faux pas among Hindutva extremists.

Then again, with the movie’s attempts to stoke religious fervor being all but cancelled out, perhaps it makes sense to approach Ramayana: Rise of a Legend as a hermetically sealed experience, if only for a moment. This doesn’t do it any favors either; aesthetically, it’s a headache. While the grandeur of the costumes and archaic sets might draw one’s gaze in static moments, the cumulative effect of its many action scenes is often bewildering. Sure, some individual shots are big and bold in composition, but they’re frenetic and unfocused when strung together, with points of interest shifting in and out, left and right, and up and down at random. The fantasy fights lack the necessary physical and emotional weight to make a lasting impact, even before Rama’s fellow exiled brother Lakshmana (Ravi Dubey) delivers his umpteenth comic quip met by awkward dead air, adding to the Marvel-ness of it all.

Hans Zimmer and A.R. Rahman join hands for some booming compositions, but the music — much of which has been trimmed for the shorter cut, which features little singing or dancing — is tasked with scoring scenes and set pieces with no rhythm of their own. The film feels entirely off-kilter even before characters open their distracting, digitally-rendered mouths. That some of them sound like whiny, posh South Bombay youngsters is, perhaps, something only viewers who’ve spent time in India might really notice in the English version, but it nonetheless robs the dialogue-heavy drama of its potential grandeur.

There are fleeting pluses to be found throughout the runtime. The movie, for instance, features one moment of evocative suggestion — a harsh cut from a nightmarish vision to an empty throne, denoting the weight of Rama’s regal responsibility — but anything resembling moral dilemma is quickly swallowed by prescriptive plot developments, which leave little room for meaningful character arcs or emotional possibility. A climactic mid-air battle does, quite mercifully, manage to capture the imagination without much haphazard distraction. However, this scene doesn’t actually involve Rama at all. Its focus, instead, is a supporting character barely glimpsed up until that point, leaving Kapoor’s dreary protagonist to promise a more cogent narrative in the sequel.

The duology’s success is all but guaranteed in India and among its diaspora, given how deeply entrenched the Ramayana remains in Hindu and Indian culture (its release coincides with the major festival Diwali). However, to anyone not already attuned to the tale, it’s hard to imagine this version being a meaningful enough introduction to bring novice viewers back. It has neither the emotional hook nor the soul to compensate for its lack of visual momentum, and its reliance on a half-baked tech to appeal to a wider audience impedes what little drama exists at its core.

Read on, if you don’t want to wind up as meat for the worms…

Your Sword is much more than just a weapon

If you think a sword can only be helpful for fighting monsters, think again! In Queen’s Domain, your sword is as much for traversing the world and platforming as it is for fighting. Practice ‘dashing’ with your holy sword, and soon you’ll be able to reach faraway places.

Each battle is a dance

Those who swing their weapons around wildly won’t last long on Neasied. If you are careless and don’t pay attention to the rhythms of your opponents, you’ll be defeated by even the most trifling of enemies. Watch carefully to learn their attack patterns, study the bestiary to learn their weaknesses, and make sure you use the right weapon for each encounter. Preparation is everything!

Curiosity pays

Queen’s Domain is designed to reward curiosity. Taking the path less trodden can lead you to hidden treasures, secret paths and much more. If you’re ever really stumped, here’s a top tip: the dead sometimes leave hints for the living…

Keep your eyes open

Enemies can come at you from any angle, and often when you least expect it. Make sure you equip your favourite throwable weapon when venturing out into the world and always look around you for potential threats around and above you. Blundering into open spaces is a quick way to a grisly end.

Not just you, your weapons Level Up too!

The more you practice with a weapon, the more powerful it will become! If you get stuck at an especially difficult place, try retracing your steps and leveling up your weapon of choice. Then you can save and return with a better chance at defeating the evils around you!

Virtua Fighter Crossroads Showcases Lionel Rafale in New Trailer

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Following teases by Ryu Ga Gotoku Studio from earlier this month about a new announcement for Virtua Fighter Crossroads, the studio has now released a new trailer giving us a look at one of the title’s roster members – a returning Lionel Rafale. The trailer, which you can check out below, features 2 minutes of gameplay footage alongside some story moments.

Lionel has been described by RGG Studio as “a mysterious young martial artist” who shows up in the city of Vilasapara. Further in the description, the studio has said that he is “a highly skilled fighter capable of outclassing Cielo in a street fight,” and went on to say that “he is a force to be reckoned with.” However, his story remains a mystery, much like his true reasons for showing up in Vilasapara.

Around half of the trailer is dedicated to showcasing Lionel’s moveset, with which he is able to put his opponent on the back foot thanks to a variety of knee strikes, punches, and kicks. Lionel also has a few grapples with which he can trip his opponent, leaving them on the ground and open to more attacks. He ultimately finishes the fight by throwing his enemy against a wall and doing a high kick assisted with a single-palm handstand.

Virtua Fighter Crossroads was officially unveiled as the official title for RGG’s New Virtua Fighter Project back in June. At the time, the studio released a trailer showcasing some of the game’s visuals and its story. The core setting of Virtua Fighter Crossroads – Vilasapara – will see many fighters arriving for a massive competition. However, there are also other problems to face, including the Chinese mafia that is seemingly on the run.

In a further showcase, the studio confirmed that Virtua Fighter Crossroads won’t simply be a fighting game. While it will still feature the franchise’s traditional VS Mode, the core of the title will be an “open sandbox RPG” with an emphasis on player choice. Considering the development team’s history, it is easy to assume that this game might follow a similar path to the Yakuza/Like a Dragon and Judgment franchises in offering players a dense world to explore paired with an emotional story.

June also saw the release of a trailer showcasing the Bakunawa Killer. The character is seemingly sneaking around the city of Vilasapara and waiting for the perfect chance to take out the fighters who have decided not to participate in the tournament that will form the core of the game’s story. The Bakunawa Killer’s identity remains unknown, however, and his trailer even had Cielo and Pai Chan chasing after him. However, before his true identity could be revealed, the trailer simply comes to an end.

As for his fighting style, the Bakunawa Killer will seemingly make use of a wuxia-inspired moveset with high-impact strikes. He will likely also be capable of some intense acrobatics that will end up giving him an edge when it comes to positioning and setting up crossover attacks.

Virtua Fighter Crossroads doesn’t yet have a release date aside from a general 2027 window, and RGG Studio hasn’t even confirmed its target platforms so far.

Destiny Fans Shouldn’t Expect Any Announcement at The Game Awards, Says Head of Product

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

While there might be plenty of excitement regarding the future of the Destiny franchise following Bungie’s announcement that it will slowly bring back vaulted content into Destiny 2, the company has now said that fans shouldn’t expect any major announcement at the upcoming The Game Awards. In a new blog post, the studio spoke about its plans for the future, and the fact that it still has plenty of things it needs to figure out.

Head of product Josh Deane has confirmed that Bungie is currently in the pre-production phase of its long-term content unvaulting plans. The studio is specifically targeting content from campaigns, strikes, and raids to be brought back, along with the Menagerie and all of its activities. After this, the studio is looking at other things it could bring along as stretch goals, like more PvP maps and updates to raids and their associated weapons. And finally, Bungie might look at how it can improve the New Light experience, and we could even see things like seasonal story missions and their associated hubs return.

As for the timeline, Deane has said that the current timeline indicates that pre-production and the proof-of-concept stage will end by February, following which the full-blown production will kick off. All in all, the studio expects it to be completed in around 12 months from the first announcement.

“I think before we close it’s important to know that this is a true reset moment for Bungie, Marathon, and Destiny. We also know time isn’t our friend; we’re playing from behind with the cards we have and I’m sure we’ll have to take more punches and make some hard decisions along the way,” Deane explained. After discussing how the company has to work on its ongoing projects with different constraints and the need to allocate resources to them in appropriate ways, he went on to say that fans shouldn’t expect to see the Destiny logo randomly show up during The Game Awards.

“Knowing we are early in incubating our future and actively working on projects to bridge us to that new home, don’t expect us to make a Destiny announcement at the TGA’s this year, or to randomly drop a logo that sets the world on fire right away.”

In the meantime, Deane has also spoken about what it means for Bungie to be “not done with Destiny”. There are seemingly three things planned for the game: a “Celebration” beyond Moment of Triumph, “Legacy”, which might indicate plans to bring Destiny 1 content over to Destiny 2, and a “Redacted” thing. The last one wasn’t really detailed, with Deane simply offering a tease by saying, “Launch Ventrilo Message: ‘We’re back’”. Each of the three Destiny-related projects will get their own progress bars, with “Celebration” and “Legacy” getting theirs in February.

On the subject of “Redacted”, Destiny 2’s communications manager Dylan Gafner said that the “[I] always love when we use that.” He went on to reassert the fact that Bungie wants to be more transparent with its fans, and the progress bars for its projects are meant to be promises “we intend to keep”.

Yaga Episode 3 Recap and Review

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Warning: This review contains full spoilers for Yaga Season 1, Episode 3. Episodes 1-3 are now streaming on AMC+ in the US and Crave in Canada, with new episodes releasing on Fridays at midnight PT.

Yaga Episode 3 wastes no time diving right back into this eerie, witchy tale, while answering some of our biggest questions after last week’s debut.

We pick up right where Episode 2’s cliffhanger left off – on the beach with Logan’s body – prior to getting confirmation from the police that, yes, it is in fact Logan’s lifeless body (not Henry’s) and that blood was found near a window open in his bedroom. There’s a moment of confusion for the viewer as Rapp sees Logan’s eyes jump open, and we see that they are ice blue – like Henry’s.

By now, we know Rapp has been hallucinating more frequently and this is possibly either from the drugs from the night before (because he honestly looks like he’s still high) or something dark and ominous that’s happening to him.

We see a pattern in relation to “time” in Yaga. This is confirmed when Carson recounts what happened the previous night at Henry’s party where we learn that “a few hours” had passed between seeing Logan disappear into his room upstairs, and Carson finding Rapp on the beach. Originally, it seemed like some time had passed, but nothing substantial.

Time Blips

In Episode 1, there was a time blip between text messages Rapp received seemingly one after another in the bar scene while speaking with Katherine. His text from Carson comes in at 8:11pm, and another text – only seconds later – comes in from Angel at 9:41pm. From what we see as viewers, time has stayed the same in the environment of where he is, but something is clearly controlling time in relation to him. (I noticed this after the publishing of the premiere review, and I blame missing these subtle clues on being only human and not a witch myself.)

As we continue on with more unexplained moments in this story, Rapp tries to put a move on Carson in his hotel room, which she rejects. He then goes to lie down on his bed and falls asleep almost too quickly – Carson comes and kneels down next to the bed and we see the clock turn to 3:33 (not the first time we’ve seen this either), while intense, whispering voices begin to take over as she leans in to almost kiss him. Rapp gently opens his eyes and sees Pam, Henry’s ex-girlfriend, seductively pulling a white feather out of his mouth.

He suddenly jolts out of his actual sleep and awakens some time later in the day. We also notice a small circle tattoo on Carson’s wrist before they head over to forensics, which seems to be placed in the shot intentionally for us to refer back to later…

Going to the mortuary, we see Logan’s corpse – well, most of it. In the episode’s biggest WTF moment, we learn Logan’s penis was severed from his body and he was castrated. It turns out he was also stabbed with an ancient knife made out of Obsidian and human bone – which Katherine later classifies as an Aztec ceremonial midwife sacrificial knife, connected to deities with symbols representing sexual desire and fertility. All signs so far point to Logan being murdered, and Carson and the police on staff are searching for more evidence, especially as this new case potentially relates to Henry.

We see Carson walk up to a small church to meet Logan’s mom, Alice (played by Sarah Strange). The dark, moody shots and heavy music come together to create an emotionally beautiful scene of grief between the two women. They sit on a bench in the cemetery together and more is revealed about Logan that we hadn’t known before. That includes Logan changing his hair and working out constantly – like he was trying to become Henry himself, perhaps.

Alice reveals her hate for the Parks and shares how she heard a rumor that Logan and Henry bought one of Pam’s lame hens from her and joked they were going to sacrifice it. She then explains passionately how she could see everything affecting Logan, saying, “Everyday, I’d see that thing growing in him. That thing getting bigger and thicker and darker and pulling him further and further away from God, and closer and closer to the Devil.” Carson asks about Logan’s dad, but Alice dismisses the questions.

While Carson is with Alice, we see Rapp jogging on the beach. Pam happens to be running in front of him. He tries to catch up to her and, as he does, she all of a sudden splits into three versions of herself, distorted in Rapp’s view. She calls his name and he trips over a log, seeing more visions and a sinister mask staring back at him, as he peers up at Pam who is somehow immediately next to him with a cheeky comment.

Before Carson and Rapp decide to head back over to Henry’s house to check for more leads, Carson inquires if Henry and Logan were possibly in a relationship, giving Rapp unintentional fuel to speculate on Carson’s sexuality as well, which she avoids divulging.

"The Maiden, the Mother, and the Crone."

As the duo begin surveying Logan’s room, you can see the pile of bloodied sheets next to the window, and they begin slowly uncovering more and more clues as they go along. Interestingly, they find drug powder in a canister with the same triangle symbol we saw from the previous episodes – a symbol known as the Triskelion – which has multiple interpretations. In Celtic mythology it is said to represent sacred femininity and the Triple Goddess archetype: the Maiden, the Mother, and the Crone. Baba Yaga in Slavic folklore is known as the “Crone,” so we’ll have to wait and see how that relates (if at all) as the series continues.

Carson and Rapp also notice the chicken foot hanging from Logan’s lampshade, previously in Henry’s room – and we can now pretty safely assume it’s from the chicken he and Henry were rumored to have sacrificed.

Just when they are trying to figure out how Logan disappeared from his room the night before, Rapp discovers the bookshelf is actually a secret door into another room… a small, eerie spot illuminated by red lights, despite the rest of the house having no power. A desk with a tape recorder and an archive of dozens upon dozens of cassette tapes are stacked on shelves.

As they dig in further, Carson and Rapp find out there’s a recording from the previous night in Henry’s bedroom – from when Logan was having sex with the woman wearing the angel wings. We hear Logan demanding to be called “Henry,” making us wonder if Henry was even at the party last night – despite his phone being traced to that location.

Rapp searches through Henry’s room, discovering – disturbingly – a wired bug hidden on his nightstand, which is triggered to record in Logan’s “secret office” whenever someone speaks in the room. Carson plays more of the recording from the prior night and they discover Pam is the one who was having sex with Logan, just hours before his death – making her officially a suspect in Logan’s murder.

As we’re processing that surge of information, Rapp’s eyes suddenly catch on a drop of blood on the floor next to the bed – he drops down and peers under, seeing something bloody smeared on the floor. He quickly pulls the bed back, pushing it out of the way, and as Carson comes into the room we see the Triskelion symbol drawn wide across the floor in blood, with three white chicken feathers near each point in the symbol – seemingly signifying a curse or some kind of spell. It’s even more eerie with the haunting music, which pulls you into its claws and feels like a spell wrapping itself tightly around you, right as Baba Yaga narrates through the end of the episode.

We see solid character development in this episode, with Clark Backo stepping more into her role as Carson and raising the question of where her boundary lies between professionalism and intrigue. Noah Reid as Rapp brings more grit to his role and shows a darker, more intense side to his character – I’m looking forward to seeing how both of these characters expand into this world from here.

Some lingering questions we now have going into Episode 4: Do Henry and Logan have the same dad and is that why Alice despises the Parks so much? Is Pam a witch or maybe Baba Yaga in disguise? Did Logan put the spell under Henry’s bed, and if so, why and who’s really behind it?
